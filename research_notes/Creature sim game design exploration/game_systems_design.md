## 1. Soft lifespan pressure: what makes a designed species decline after ~3–7 real days without feeling arbitrary, how is it tuned, and how is "survival" scored fairly?

### Takeaway
The best-grounded, most legible soft pressure is **enemy-mediated negative frequency/density dependence** ("kill the winner", Janzen–Connell, Red Queen): specialist parasites/pathogens/predators that accumulate against whatever is abundant and has been around longest — it is real ecology, it has been reproduced in digital evolution (Avida, Tierra), and it scales with success so it reads as fair. Mutation load (Muller's ratchet) and boom-bust resource overshoot are real but work better as secondary "finishers"; shipped games mostly solve the same problem with hard seasonal resets (≈1–3 months), which is a useful outer wrapper but not the soft per-species pressure the designer wants.

### Cited Findings

**Biology: enemies that specialise on the abundant ("kill the winner", Red Queen, Janzen–Connell)**
- In the "kill the winner" (KtW) model of microbial ecology, abundant prokaryotic types face the heaviest viral pressure, which stops any one type from dominating; negative frequency-dependent selection (NFDS) is its most prominent aspect, though the authors stress KtW also rests on a trade-off between *competition specialists* and *defence specialists*, and the "winner" is the competition specialist, not necessarily the most abundant population — [Winter et al., "killing the winner hypothesis revisited", c. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2832346)
- Parasites that target common host genotypes give rare genotypes an advantage; in a 2021 simulation study, high host diversity persisted when parasite-mediated selection was stronger than host fecundity selection — but the same authors caution that the correlation between infection prevalence and diversity can be weak even when parasites are the only force maintaining diversity (i.e. the mechanism can be working without being visible in simple stats) — [Lively et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8692037)
- There is experimental (not just theoretical) evidence for NFDS during host–parasite coevolution in a freshwater snail / sterilising trematode system (title-level evidence only) — [Evolution 63(9), 2009](https://complete.bioone.org/journals/evolution/volume-63/issue-9/j.1558-5646.2009.00711.x/Evidence-for-Negative-Frequency-Dependent-Selection-During-Experimental-Coevolution-of/10.1111/j.1558-5646.2009.00711.x.full)
- A stochastic KtW model found that **demographic noise destroys the deterministic coexistence state through a cascade of extinctions**; letting predators and prey coevolve by mutation avoids the cascade and preserves diversity — [Xue & Goldenfeld, Phys. Rev. Lett. 2017](https://link.aps.org/accepted/10.1103/PhysRevLett.119.268101)
- Janzen–Connell hypothesis: specialist natural enemies (herbivores, pathogens) reduce survival of a species' seeds/seedlings near adults or at high conspecific density, leaving room for rarer species. A 2014 meta-analysis found survival significantly reduced near conspecifics and at high conspecific density, with stronger effects at the seedling stage; effect sizes varied widely and the authors say more work is needed to show it actually drives coexistence — [Comita et al. 2014, J. Ecology (Oxford repository record)](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/pubs:447677); [Wikipedia overview](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Janzen-Connell_hypothesis)
- Direct experiment: in a tropical forest, fungicide treatment significantly *reduced* seedling diversity (pathogens were maintaining it); insecticide did not change diversity but greatly increased recruitment and shifted species composition — [Bagchi et al., Nature 2014](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1038/nature12911)
- Predators that *switch* to whichever prey is common produce frequency-dependent predation that protects rare prey; a lionfish study found Type II responses to single prey but switching when several prey were offered, and suggested prey extirpations are less likely in diverse communities — [McCard et al., Biological Invasions 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10530-021-02487-7). A lab insect experiment reported that frequency-dependent predation tied to predator *learning* greatly enhanced prey coexistence, with long-lasting coexistence and periodic oscillations — [summary of the study](https://peeref.com/works/15114414)
- Van Valen's "law of constant extinction" (1973): most taxonomic groups showed exponentially decaying survivorship curves, i.e. the probability of extinction of a taxon is effectively independent of its age; his Red Queen explanation is continual coevolution among many species in a community. The age-independence claim and the mechanism are disputed in later reviews (extinction rates have not been constant through time; little evidence of direct competition at genus/family level) — [review, PMC6012711](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6012711); [Biological Theory 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13752-021-00391-w); [Digital Atlas of Ancient Life](https://www.digitalatlasofancientlife.org/learn/evolution/macroevolution/abiotic-vs-biotic-causes-of-macroevolution/)

**Biology: mutation load, boom–bust, fluctuation**
- Muller's ratchet: in finite asexual populations the least-mutated genotype class is lost by drift and cannot be recovered; successive "clicks" lower fitness. Lynch et al. (1993) extended this to **mutational meltdown** — the ratchet reduces population size, which accelerates the ratchet, which further reduces population size, ending in extinction — [Wikipedia: Muller's ratchet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muller%27s_ratchet); [Lynch, Bürger, Butcher & Gabriel 1993](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/5136/1/038.pdf); [Olofsson et al., "Mutational meltdown in asexual populations doomed to extinction"](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/448563.full.pdf)
- The ratchet turns faster in *small* populations; meltdowns are considered much less important in sexual species with effective sizes above a few hundred; and not every asexual lineage goes extinct (small amounts of compensating processes can stabilise even small populations) — [Wikipedia: Muller's ratchet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muller%27s_ratchet); [PMC3416009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3416009)
- Paradox of enrichment (Rosenzweig 1971): raising prey carrying capacity destabilises predator–prey equilibria into limit cycles whose troughs approach zero, where random fluctuation causes extinction of predator, prey or both. Empirical support is mainly from small lab experiments, with limited field support — [Wikipedia: Paradox of enrichment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradox_of_enrichment); [Feng & Li, arXiv 2011](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1104.4355)
- Fluctuating environments: Hutchinson's 1961 "paradox of the plankton" (many species coexist on few resources) is commonly resolved by non-equilibrium mechanisms — weather-driven fluctuations reverse competitive hierarchies before exclusion completes; modern coexistence theory names the *storage effect* and *relative nonlinearity* as the fluctuation-dependent mechanisms — [plankton review](https://sidalc.net/search/Record/dig-wur-nl-wurpubs-321100); [modern coexistence theory review, arXiv 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.07926v1.pdf)
- The Intermediate Disturbance Hypothesis is contested: Fox (2013) reports that a review of 100+ published diversity–disturbance relationships found the predicted peak in fewer than 20% of studies and argues it should be abandoned; Roxburgh/Shea et al. (2004) treat it as a family of mechanisms rather than one — [Fox 2013](https://typeset.io/pdf/the-intermediate-disturbance-hypothesis-should-be-abandoned-2hdlp3utm0.pdf); [Roxburgh, Shea & Wilson](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/the-intermediate-disturbance-hypothesis-patch-dynamics-and-mechan/)

**Digital-evolution evidence that the enemy mechanism works in a simulation**
- In Avida, parasites raised host diversity *whether or not mutations were switched on*; the mechanism identified is negative frequency-dependent selection imposed by the parasites — [BEACON Center blog on Zaman et al.](https://beacon-center.org/?p=676); [Zaman et al., GECCO 2011](https://unpaywall.org/10.1145%2F2001576.2001607)
- In Avida, host–parasite coevolution produced more complex hosts than evolution without parasites; parasite populations "retain a genetic record of past coevolutionary states", so hosts escape by performing progressively more complex functions, and coevolved hosts became more evolvable — [Zaman et al., PLoS Biology 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4267771)
- In Tierra (Ray, 1991) parasites were never coded in: they emerged, followed by immune hosts, parasites that circumvented immunity, hyper-parasites that subverted parasites and drove them toward extinction, "social" hosts, and cheating hyper-hyper-parasites — [Ray, Tierra paper](https://faculty.cc.gatech.edu/~turk/bio_sim/articles/tierra_thomas_ray.pdf); [Univ. of Delaware Messenger, 1992](https://www1.udel.edu/PR/Messenger/92/2/16.html)
- Karl Sims' competing virtual creatures (1994): when populations co-evolve in one-on-one contests over a shared resource, "interesting and diverse strategies and counter-strategies emerge" — [Sims 1994 (bibliographic record)](https://gpbib.cs.ucl.ac.uk/gp-html/alife94_sims.html); [karlsims.com](https://karlsims.com/evolved-virtual-creatures.html)

**Game analogues: how shipped games bound the life of a "run" or a world**
- Path of Exile challenge leagues run on roughly a 13-week / 3–4-month cycle; everyone starts with nothing, and characters migrate to the permanent Standard league at the end. The rationale given in design commentary (fan/analyst sources, not official GGG statements) is controlled scarcity — persistent economies drift to inflation, saturation and erosion of reward meaning; Standard "has accumulated years of inflation and wealth concentration" — [PoE wiki: League](https://www.poewiki.net/wiki/League); [PoE2 wiki: League](https://poe2wiki.net/wiki/League); [Game-Wisdom analysis](https://game-wisdom.com/general/design-philosophy-behind-poe-2s-seasonal-model-resetting-everything-works)
- Screeps (persistent programming MMO) added a separate *Seasonal World*: a ~two-month temporary server run by the devs; the original draft proposed 2–3-month seasons with a wipe each season, and the team may change constants or the map generator per season — [ScreepsPlus wiki](https://wiki.screepspl.us/Subscription); [Screeps forum: "Draft: Seasonal server"](https://screeps.com/forum/post/14704)
- A Screeps season announcement states every season has different rules and (Season #5) starts from a new empty world where every player has 100 CPU — i.e. the reset is also used to equalise budgets (attribution to this URL is from the search summary; verify) — [Steam news mirror](https://eprison.de/spiele/screeps-world/steam-news/5151600576595511795/8014/1014.html)
- Rust forces a map wipe on every server with the monthly update on the first Thursday of each month; blueprint (progression) wipes are less frequent and sources disagree on policy. Sources are third-party server hosts, not Facepunch — [runonflux wipe schedule](https://rust.runonflux.com/wipe-schedule); [gghost explainer](https://www.gghost.games/knowledgebase/85/Rust-wipe-schedule-what-force-wipe-day-does-to-your-server-and-how-to-handle-it.html)
- Neptune's Pride matches take 2–6 weeks of real time (Kyburz: "when your game takes 2-6 weeks to play it takes a while to find the bugs and get your feedback") — [Game Developer interview with Iron Helmet](https://gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/interview-space-lions-iron-helmet-talks-i-neptune-s-pride-i-); [NP help](https://np.ironhelmet.com/help/intro)
- Subterfuge deliberately shortened that: designed to be played "a few minutes here and there over the course of a week or two"; Ron Carmel liked Neptune's Pride's cadence but found month-long matches too demanding — [MCV interview](https://www.mcvuk.com/we-wanted-to-minimise-the-number-of-times-per-day-a-player-has-to-check-in/); [Game Developer, "A Brawl and a Race"](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/-a-brawl-and-a-race-designing-for-the-long-game-in-i-subterfuge-i-)
- Teamfight Tactics uses periodic partial rotations (about every three months, per the search summary) to remove problematic traits/units, and Riot states it wants a wide variety of comps to be viable each patch — [Riot: TFT Set 1 learnings, 2019](https://nexus.leagueoflegends.com/en-us/2019/10/dev-tft-set-1-learnings/); [Riot: design pillars of TFT](https://teamfighttactics.leagueoflegends.com/en-gb/news/dev/dev-design-pillars-of-tft)
- Dwarf Fortress's unofficial motto "Losing is fun" has its own Wikipedia redirect — the canonical precedent for framing inevitable collapse as the point — [Wikipedia: Losing is fun → Dwarf Fortress](https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/Losing_is_fun)

### Inferences
- **Mechanism ranking for this game (my assessment):**
  1. **Specialist-enemy accumulation ("blight clock").** Each released species accrues a species-specific enemy (parasite/pathogen/predator search-image) whose effectiveness grows with the species' cumulative abundance × time. Grounding: KtW, Janzen–Connell, Avida/Tierra parasites. Properties: tunable (adaptation rate, transmission ∝ density), fair (pressure ∝ success; a dominant species burns faster), legible ("a parasite strain has specialised on your species — 34% infected"), and story-generating. Trade-off: must be visible in the UI, since Lively et al. warn the effect can be statistically invisible in simple prevalence stats.
  2. **Predator learning / prey switching** by NPC generalists and by other players' programmed species: abundant prey become the default target. Cheap to implement if species have recognisable signals; complements (1).
  3. **Environmental drift** (season/climate cycle on a period comparable to the target lifespan): a design tuned to launch-day conditions slowly mismatches. Grounding: storage effect / non-equilibrium coexistence. Risk: feels arbitrary if the player cannot forecast it — publish a forecast.
  4. **Resource overshoot / boom–bust** (paradox of enrichment): very efficient consumers crash their own food. Good as an emergent punisher of "too good" designs but chaotic; hard to tune to a duration.
  5. **Mutation load / inbreeding (Muller's ratchet, meltdown).** Real, but theory says it bites mainly *small asexual* populations; best used as an end-game "extinction vortex" so that a species below N individuals fades decisively instead of lingering for days — not as the primary clock.
  6. **Hard season wipe** (PoE/Screeps/Rust): reliable outer loop for resetting the world and equalising players; not a soft per-species pressure.
- **Why the pressure must ramp rather than be constant (worked numbers, my calculation).** A constant extinction hazard (Van Valen-style exponential survivorship) with a 5-day mean gives a 10th–90th percentile lifespan of ~0.5 to ~11.5 days — far too spread to feel like "about 3–7 days", and dominated by luck. To get 10% of species dead by day 3 and 90% by day 7 (median ≈ 5.0 d), lifespans need roughly a Weibull distribution with shape ≈ 3.6 and scale ≈ 5.6 days, i.e. a hazard rising about as (age)^2.6 — about 18× higher on day 6 than day 2. Enemy adaptation that compounds with cumulative host-days naturally produces this ramp; a flat upkeep/decay tax does not.
- **Tuning by feedback control rather than hand-set constants.** Because population sizes will be small (5–20 players' species) and Xue & Goldenfeld show demographic noise causes extinction cascades in exactly this regime, a global "virulence" coefficient can be auto-adjusted so the trailing median lifespan stays near the target (analogous in spirit to RimWorld's storyteller managing pacing, Section 5). This is a design proposal, not a sourced practice.
- **Escape valves keep it soft.** Avida results (hosts escape parasites by acquiring new functions) suggest the counter-play: a mid-life tweak or a rare-morph offspring partially resets enemy adaptation, with diminishing returns — so skilled play extends life toward day 7+ without making immortality possible (see Section 8 on mid-life edits).
- **Scoring fairness (design reasoning).** (a) Score per *release* ("run"), not per account, so a player who joins in week 6 competes immediately; (b) rank on a robust composite — e.g. lifespan in days plus "biomass-days" (area under the population curve) — because peak biomass rewards boom–bust designs that the pressure system is meant to punish, and pure lifespan rewards tiny hidden populations; (c) keep both "best run this season" and "median of last N runs" boards so one lucky run doesn't dominate; (d) wrap everything in seasons that reset the world (PoE/Screeps precedent) so early joiners' incumbency doesn't compound. Descendant counts only make sense if designs can speciate (Section 2).

### Gaps

- No empirical evidence found on how players *perceive* soft, ecology-driven decline versus hard caps; the fairness/legibility claims above are inference.
- I did not retrieve primary texts for Van Valen (1973), Rosenzweig (1971) or Lynch et al. (1993); statements come from secondary summaries. PoE and Rust rationale come from fan wikis, analysts and server hosts rather than the developers.

## 2. Sexual vs asexual reproduction, mutation and drift: should player designs be fixed or evolve in the wild?

### Takeaway
The surveyed ALife games sit at two poles — fully evolving species with no authorship (The Bibites, Species: ALRE) or player-authored species that are *shielded* from automatic evolution (Thrive) — and none retrieved shows a successful middle where a designed species freely drifts yet stays "yours". Theory says free mutation in small asexual populations degrades rather than improves lineages, and digital-evolution results show unconstrained evolution finds exploits (parasitism, cheating) that undermine authored intent.

### Cited Findings
- The Bibites: creatures have brains that "can evolve in logic and topology alongside their genetic code"; random mutation can spawn a new brain connection linking two neurons; the game features a realistic energy system plus speciation and lineage trees — [The Bibites (itch.io)](https://thebibites.itch.io/the-bibites); [Bibites wiki](https://the-bibites.fandom.com/wiki/Bibites)
- The Bibites 0.6.0 devlog: species "are plentiful and often come in bursts of speciation", and the game prunes old irrelevant species and erases them from the sim's history to manage data size; tracked metrics include brain-size distribution — [Bibites 0.6.0 devlog](https://thebibites.itch.io/the-bibites/devlog/809631/the-bibites-060-organs-and-science)
- Thrive separates the two systems: "auto-evo" evolves the world's other species (random mutations per NPC species, keep the best outcome), while the player evolves their own species manually in the editor; the original algorithm explicitly *overrode* auto-evo results for the player species because the player could not predict how editor changes would play out — [Thrive Devblog #20](https://revolutionarygamesstudio.com/devblog-20-lets-try-to-solve-evolution/); [Thrive wiki talk page (old)](https://thrive.fandom.com/wiki/Talk:Automatic_evolution?oldid=7687)
- Thrive later moved toward exposing the player to simulation consequences without taking over their design: a patch added a prediction panel showing how auto-evo evaluates the player's species in the editor, and a January 2025 update raised the strength of auto-evo's population effect on the player from 20% to 50% and made it a difficulty option — [Thrive patch 0.5.6.1](https://itch.io/devlog/323660/patch-0561.amp); [Thrive progress update 2025-01-25](https://revolutionarygamesstudio.com/progress-update-01-25-2025/)
- Species: Artificial Life, Real Evolution has no authored species at all: creatures inherit genes with random changes and speciate under mutation and selection; the player's levers are indirect — a nursery (isolated area to tinker with the gene pool), rovers to decide which creatures survive, and global catastrophes (ice age, flood); analysis tools include a clade diagram, satellite map and web-of-life view — [Steam store page](https://store.steampowered.com/app/774541?l=italian)
- Muller's ratchet / mutational meltdown (see Section 1): asexual lineages in small populations accumulate deleterious mutations irreversibly; the effect is much weaker in sexual populations above a few hundred individuals — [Wikipedia: Muller's ratchet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muller%27s_ratchet)
- Digital organisms reproduce asexually, so Avida researchers define "species" functionally (phenotypically/ecologically distinct groups) rather than by reproductive isolation — [Chow et al., Science 2004](https://www.rifters.com/real/articles/Science_DigitalOrganisms.pdf)
- Unconstrained digital evolution finds strategies the designer never authored: Tierra spontaneously evolved parasites, hyper-parasites and cheaters — [Univ. of Delaware Messenger, 1992](https://www1.udel.edu/PR/Messenger/92/2/16.html)
- Polyworld (Yaeger, 1994) treated mutation rate, life span and the fraction of energy given to offspring as heritable agent traits, and supported sexual reproduction — [Yaeger, ALife III paper](https://shinyverse.org/larryy/Yaeger.ALife3.pdf)
- Karl Sims' creatures were bred by copying, combining and randomly mutating genes of the most successful individuals — [karlsims.com](https://karlsims.com/evolved-virtual-creatures.html)

### Inferences
- **Three workable positions, with trade-offs:**
  - *Fixed clonal designs (no mutation).* Clean authorship and scoring; lifespan is governed entirely by external pressure (Section 1), which makes the 3–7-day target easiest to tune. Cost: no in-world adaptation, the daily check-in has less to show.
  - *Bounded micro-variation.* Offspring jitter numeric parameters (size, speed, thresholds, synapse weights) within a small envelope around the design, under the same budget cap. Gives visible local adaptation, lineage trees and rare "escape" morphs against specialist enemies while keeping the species recognisably the player's. Likely the best fit for "Tamagotchi × Spore".
  - *Open-ended mutation/speciation.* Maximum emergence (Bibites, Species) but conflicts with three stated goals: authorship ("your species evolved away from your design"), the fixed budget (mutations must be budget-checked or the cap erodes), and the lifespan target (adaptive lineages can outrun the pressure; and per Bibites, speciation bursts create data-volume problems).
- **Make evolvability itself a costed design choice.** Polyworld-style heritable mutation rate, exposed as a designer-set dial that costs budget, turns "fixed vs evolving" into a strategy axis rather than a global rule: high mutation = better enemy escape but mutational load in small populations (Muller's ratchet gives the downside for free).
- **Sexual reproduction as a sidegrade unlock.** Biology gives a principled trade: sex costs mate-finding and halves per-capita growth, but resists the ratchet and generates rare genotypes that escape specialist parasites. That is a natural "variety not power" unlock (Section 3).
- **Attribution rule if drift is allowed.** Follow Thrive's lead: the simulation may change a species' *population*, and may show predictions, but structural changes to the design remain player-initiated; any drifted lineage is credited to the original designer through the lineage tree.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve The Bibites' genetics documentation (mutation-rate genes, reproduction mode) — two targeted searches returned nothing relevant — nor any account of what happens to *hand-designed* bibites after long runs. Whether designed creatures there are typically out-competed by their own mutants is unverified.
- No source found on Species: ALRE long-run dynamics (drift, mass extinction frequency) beyond the store description.
- No source retrieved on sexual-reproduction implementations in these games, or on player reactions to "my species evolved away from my design".

## 3. Progression and content drip: steady unlocks for a small non-monetised group without making veterans strictly stronger

### Takeaway
The proven pattern is "meta-progression as variety, not power" (Slay the Spire, Guild Wars 2's no-gear-treadmill stance, Magic's managed power level) combined with discovery-triggered unlocks (Spore's part collection, Cell Lab's challenge-gated cell types) and periodic rule variation (Screeps seasons, PoE league mechanics). The main long-term risk in horizontal systems is not power creep but **complexity creep** and loss of goals.

### Cited Findings
- Spore (2008) creature stage: new parts are unlocked by rummaging through skeleton/bone piles and by killing or befriending alpha creatures; defeated competitors often drop parts; which parts you get depends partly on what you already have; a strategy guide counts 228 collectible parts; unlocked parts become usable in the editor at the next mating call — [Spore wiki: Creature Stage](https://spore.fandom.com/wiki/Creature_Stage); [Spore wiki: Creature Creator](https://spore.fandom.com/wiki/Creature_Creator); [GameSpot hands-on](https://gamespot.com/articles/spore-exclusive-hands-on-from-cell-to-civilization/1100-6195809/); [StrategyWiki](https://strategywiki.org/wiki/Spore/Achievements)
- Cell Lab: Evolution Sandbox gates content behind challenges: e.g. the "Algae" challenge unlocks the Phagocyte gene, "Decimation" → Keratinocyte, "Slippery Slope" → Nitrocyte, "Harsh Conditions" → Virocyte, "Infection" → Secrocyte, "Population Bottleneck" → Neurocyte and programmable genome parameters, "Balloons" → Myocyte, "Friction" → Senseocyte/Stereocyte. Source is a Korean fan wiki (snapshot June 2026) — [namu.moe: Cell Lab](https://namu.moe/w/%EC%85%80%EB%9E%A9)
- Slay the Spire: a design-analysis site characterises Mega Crit's approach as meta-progression that unlocks *more options, not more strength*, contrasted with models where each run permanently raises starting power; difficulty is layered separately via the Ascension staircase. (Fan analysis paraphrasing interviews — developer intent unverified.) — [game-mechanics wiki: "meta as variety, not power"](https://saschb2b.github.io/game-mechanics/concepts/meta-as-variety-not-power); [Ascension page](https://saschb2b.github.io/game-mechanics/games/slay-the-spire/ascension)
- Slay the Spire's early card/relic unlocks function as a gradual tutorial: each character starts with a good selection and unlocks the rest "rather quickly" — [Hamatti notes](https://notes.hamatti.org/Gaming/Video-games/Meta-progression-with-gradual-tutorial-in-roguelike-games)
- Guild Wars 2: the official wiki states the game lacks a gear treadmill; progression comes through account-wide features and masteries — [GW2 wiki: Endgame](https://wiki.guildwars2.com/wiki/Endgame)
- Costs of horizontal progression reported by commentators/players: it is very beginner-friendly but players "quickly lose their goals"; and "it's really hard to have horizontal progression and not constantly increase complexity… vertical, it's easy, you introduce new gear tiers" (forum opinion) — [Mein-MMO](https://mein-mmo.de/en/3-things-that-all-other-mmorpgs-should-learn-from-guild-wars-2-and-3-mistakes-they-should-avoid,692897); [GW2 forum thread](https://en-forum.guildwars2.com/topic/134306-i-just-realised-yet-another-relics-rant/page/2)
- When ArenaNet added the Ascended gear tier, many players felt it betrayed the horizontal design — evidence that a community treats "no vertical creep" as a promise — [PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/au/arenanet-guild-wars-2-gear-debate)
- Magic: The Gathering R&D manages perceived power with the "Escher stairwell" — raising some cards' power while lowering others so power appears to climb while staying roughly level; Mark Rosewater is reported to call **complexity creep** "the biggest danger to the game" and unavoidable over the long haul; rotation only partly solves it. (Secondary sources.) — [MTG Wiki: Power creep](https://mtg.wiki/page/Power_creep); [MTG Wiki: Complexity creep](https://mtg.wiki/page/Complexity_creep); [Commander's Herald](https://commandersherald.com/six-examples-of-power-creep-in-magic/)
- Path of Exile uses each league as a content testbed: many permanent features (strongboxes, essences, breaches, tormented spirits) began as league mechanics — [poedb: Challenge Leagues](https://poedb.tw/us/ChallengeLeagues)
- Screeps seasons each have different rules (see Section 1) — [Screeps forum: Draft seasonal server](https://screeps.com/forum/post/14704)
- Robocraft illustrates the vertical alternative the designer wants to avoid: a later progression system gave each garage bay its own CPU limit, starting at 750 and upgradable to 2,000, explicitly to raise "the size and complexity of robots that players can build as they progress" — [Robocraft devblog: Agility and CPU Progression System](https://robocraftgame.com/devblog/agility-and-cpu-progression-system)
- Avida: a diverse pool of *limiting resources* was sufficient to cause adaptive radiation into distinct coexisting types — i.e. new resource types create new niches — [Chow et al. 2004, abstract](https://scilit.com/publications/7c2bb288f8c72cd60844b1823d7c480d)

### Inferences
- **Rules that keep unlocks horizontal under a fixed cap:** (1) every unlock is priced at par in the same body+brain budget; (2) an unlock should open a *niche or verb* (a new resource to eat, sense to use, signal to send, relationship to form) rather than raise a stat ceiling; (3) the "kill the winner" pressure of Section 1 punishes any unlock that becomes ubiquitous, giving automatic meta-rotation; (4) unlock *breadth* can be capped per design (e.g. a "complexity meter" separate from the budget, as Spore did — Section 4) to contain complexity creep.
- **Unlock triggers that fit an ecosystem game** (pattern from Spore/Cell Lab): observation/achievement — "your species survived a winter → hibernation gland", "was parasitised for 48 h → immune module", "went extinct to starvation → fat storage"; fossils of extinct species (yours and friends') as a research currency. Extinction then *pays out*, which reframes the designed-in death as progress ("losing is fun").
- **Newcomer parity in a friend group:** make discoveries **group-wide after a short exclusivity window** (the discoverer gets first use for one release), so the group's catalogue grows together and a late joiner inherits the whole catalogue; give the discoverer cosmetic/naming credit instead of lasting power. This is a proposal; no retrieved game does exactly this.
- **Rotating rules as systemic content:** weekly world "mutators" (drought week, long nights, new biome opens, a migrating NPC megafauna) in the manner of Screeps seasons/PoE leagues give variety with almost no authored content, and they shift which existing parts are good.
- **How much content (rough arithmetic, my estimate, not sourced):** at ~1 release per player per week and a wish for 1–3 new toys per release, six months needs on the order of 30–75 atomic unlocks. Combinatorics do most of the work: 30 parts already give 435 pairs and 4,060 triples; 60 parts give 1,770 pairs and 34,220 triples. Prioritise parts that *interact* (sense × signal × behaviour function × resource) over stand-alone stat parts, and parameterised parts (one "limb" with length/number dials) over many fixed variants. AI-assisted authoring is most useful for names/descriptions/art of parameter-generated parts and for writing unlock-trigger flavour, not for inventing mechanics.
- **Watch for the documented horizontal-progression failure modes:** goal loss (add collection/"field guide" completion and seasonal titles) and complexity creep (rotate some parts out per season, MTG-style, or gate advanced neural functions behind a per-design complexity cap).

### Gaps
- No sourced figures on how much content sustains months of play, or on unlock cadence in comparable small-group games; the arithmetic above is mine.
- Not sourced (search budget): Hades-style power meta-progression, idle-game prestige, research/"fossil" currencies, daily/weekly rotating mutators in other games, card-game "sidegrade" design essays, Daniel Cook / Lost Garden material (one search returned nothing relevant), Raph Koster, Soren Johnson.
- Slay the Spire developer intent is from a fan analysis site; GW2 and MTG claims are from wikis, fan sites and forum posts rather than first-party design statements.

## 4. Fixed budget design: pricing body vs brain, avoiding dominant strategies, and rebalancing live

### Takeaway
Fixed-cap builders work when the cap is a *single shared currency with a separate complexity limit* (Spore) and when the cap is equal for everyone (Robocraft's flat 2,000 CPU; Screeps' season with 100 CPU each). Biology offers a ready-made rule for brain pricing — neural tissue is cheap to "buy" but very expensive to *run* — and both ecology and live games suggest balancing by **frequency-dependent pressure and data-driven nudges** rather than by raising caps.

### Cited Findings
- Spore's Creature Creator (2008) used two limits: a limited DNA-point budget to buy parts ("to keep you from creating the ridiculously advantaged creature") and a separate **complexity meter** which, when full, stops further parts being added; reviewers found the complexity allowance generous. Each part modifies stats (hands → striking/posing/grasping; feet → speed/dance/charge) — [Giant Bomb user review](https://www.giantbomb.com/spore-creature-creator/3030-20746/user-reviews/2200-5254/); [Spore wiki: Creature Creator](https://spore.fandom.com/wiki/Creature_Creator)
- Chaim Gingold (lead designer on Spore's editors) described the creature editor as the hardest design challenge and framed the tools as "magic crayons" aiming at soft mastery — [Game Developer: Gingold on Spore's 'magic crayon' approach](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/gingold-talks-i-spore-i-s-magic-crayon-approach)
- Robocraft: robots could be built freely as long as they stayed under a CPU limit of 2,000, and that limit did not increase with player level (Wikipedia's description). Balance was repeatedly patched around the cap — maximum Power depended on CPU (the bar always recharges in 10 s, so power regenerates 25% faster at 1,750 CPU and 25% slower at 500 CPU), and a "Damage Boost" curve by CPU was reworked "to bring a better balance to Robot damage at all CPU ranges". The studio closed and servers were announced for shutdown in January 2025 — [Wikipedia: Robocraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robocraft); [Robocraft wiki: Power](https://robocraft.gamepedia.com/Power); [mod DB news: Damage Boost update](https://www.moddb.com/news/damage-boost-crf-20-update-out-now)
- Screeps budgets *computation*: each player has a server-side CPU limit with a rollover "bucket"; one seasonal ruleset gave every player the same 100 CPU — [Screeps docs: CPU limit](https://docs.screeps.com/cpu-limit.html); [Steam news mirror](https://eprison.de/spiele/screeps-world/steam-news/5151600576595511795/8014/1014.html)
- The Bibites charges an ongoing **brain upkeep cost**; a v0.4.1 balance change lowered it specifically "to promote brain size", showing the brain-cost coefficient is a primary tuning lever for whether complex brains appear at all. Later versions added organ trade-offs (stomach, fat reserves as long-term energy storage) — [Bibites v0.4.1 patch notes](https://thebibites.itch.io/the-bibites/devlog/362871/the-bibites-v041); [Bibites 0.6.0 devlog](https://thebibites.itch.io/the-bibites/devlog/809631/the-bibites-060-organs-and-science)
- Expensive Tissue Hypothesis (Aiello & Wheeler, 1995): brain tissue is metabolically very costly (≈11.2 W/kg, reported as roughly 22× resting skeletal muscle; the snippet's muscle figure does not exactly reproduce that ratio — check the primary), a human brain runs at about 14.6 W versus ~3.0 W for an average mammal brain of equivalent body size, and the hypothesis is that a larger brain was paid for by a smaller gut. It remains debated — a later study argued fat storage matters more than gut size — [Brains and guts in human evolution (review)](https://www.scielo.br/j/bjg/a/FxXZ7LPBDmZxjKKVbPym4Vb/); [Aiello & Wheeler 1995 (PDF)](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/algernon/1995-aiello.pdf); [NBC News on the debate](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/amp/wbna45226912)
- Polyworld (1994) made size, strength, maximum speed, mutation rate, life span and offspring energy fraction heritable traits governed by a simulated physiology/metabolism, with behaviour produced by a neural network using colour vision — [Yaeger, ALife III paper](https://shinyverse.org/larryy/Yaeger.ALife3.pdf); [Ars Electronica 1993 description](https://webarchive.ars.electronica.art/en/archives/festival_archive/festival_documentations/1993/yaege.html)
- Definition used in game-balance writing: in an imbalanced game one or more "dominant strategies" quickly emerge, leaving other strategies useless — [Game Developer: Understanding balance in video games](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/understanding-balance-in-video-games)
- Live, data-driven balance precedent — Hearthstone Arena: Blizzard places cards into performance buckets using their win-rate and pick-rate and adjusts card appearance weights so predicted win rates move toward 50%, acknowledging rare cases where the result is still not ideal — [Blizzard: "Arena Balance Through Science"](https://playhearthstone.com/en-us/blog/22788308)
- Automated balancing research: LUDUS (AAAI 2022) combines automated playtesting with global search to tune auto-battler card parameters, estimating win rates by sampling line-ups (error reported negligible at group size 256); a wargame paper argues point costs are the balance lever and proposes automating cost estimation instead of manual playtests — [LUDUS review](https://liner.com/review/ludus-optimization-framework-to-balance-auto-battler-cards); [Essex: STEP-ToG24](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/39193/1/STEP-ToG24.pdf)
- Teamfight Tactics' shared champion pool is a built-in scarcity mechanism: every player draws from the same finite pool, so a unit several players want becomes hard to complete; Riot has also noted that when the most expensive units were simply the strongest, late-game boards lacked diversity — [ProGameGuides: how the TFT champion pool works](https://progameguides.com/teamfight-tactics/how-tft-champion-pool-works/); [Riot: Monsters Attack learnings](https://teamfighttactics.leagueoflegends.com/en-au/news/dev/dev-teamfight-tactics-monsters-attack-learnings/)
- Ecological analogue of anti-dominance: in Avida, with multiple limiting resources, frequency-dependent selection maintained several distinct types, and species richness peaked at *intermediate* productivity — [Chow et al. 2004](https://scilit.com/publications/7c2bb288f8c72cd60844b1823d7c480d)
- KtW theory locates coexistence in a trade-off between competition specialists and defence specialists — [Winter et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2832346)

### Inferences
- **Two-part pricing makes body and brain commensurable.** Charge every part a one-off *build cost* (counts against the fixed cap) and a running *upkeep* (energy per tick in the sim). Following the expensive-tissue logic and the Bibites lever, make neurons/functions cheap-ish to buy but costly to run, so a big brain forces a high-quality diet or a smaller gut/armour. Then "body vs brain" is felt twice: at design time (cap) and in the world (starvation risk).
- **Separate "complexity" from "cost"** as Spore did: one cap for power (budget), one for count/size of the behaviour program. That lets unlocks add variety without letting veterans stack ever-more-intricate brains, and protects phone-screen legibility and server CPU.
- **Equal caps are the fairness anchor.** Robocraft's flat cap and the Screeps 100-CPU season show the pattern; Robocraft's later per-bay CPU progression shows how quickly a cap becomes a vertical progression lever once it is allowed to grow.
- **Expect scale effects to need correction.** Robocraft had to patch power regeneration and damage by CPU bracket; in an ecosystem the analogous issue is body-size scaling (big-slow vs many-small). Use allometric upkeep (cost growing faster than size) rather than special-case curves.
- **Prefer ecological auto-balance to price changes.** The Section 1 enemy system already taxes popular designs; extending it to *parts* (a pathogen that specialises on a common organ type, a predator search-image for a common silhouette) penalises over-used parts without touching the fixed budget — thematically consistent and self-correcting, like TFT's shared pool.
- **If prices must move, do it Hearthstone-style:** small, data-driven weekly nudges from pick-rate × performance (e.g. survival-days per budget point), applied only to *future* releases (grandfather living species), with a published changelog. With 5–20 players the live sample is tiny and noisy, so server-side automated playtests (LUDUS-style batch sims of candidate designs in sandbox ecosystems) are the realistic data source.
- **Dominant-strategy smoke tests:** before adding a part, run batch sims to check that (a) no single design wins across all biomes/seasons, and (b) an all-brain and an all-body extreme both lose to mixed designs.

### Gaps
- No designer-level sources retrieved for Kerbal mass budgets, Armored Core, Besiege, Scrap Mechanic, From the Depths, deck-building cost curves or GURPS/Hero point-buy lessons; the brief's list is only partly covered (Spore, Robocraft, Screeps, Bibites).
- No retrieved example of a *live game that dynamically re-prices parts from pick/win rates in real time*; Hearthstone Arena adjusts offering rates, and the research systems are offline tuners.
- Robocraft sources do not state which specific builds were dominant or how successfully the cap prevented dominance; Cell Lab's budget mechanics were not retrieved.
- Polyworld's exact neural energy-cost model was not found in the snippets; the Bibites' numeric brain-cost values were not retrieved.
- A Soren Johnson reference on players optimising toward dominant strategies was searched for and not found.

## 5. Inter-species relationships: how they emerge or are authored, how they form over time, and how to make them legible and story-generating

### Takeaway
Rich relationships have emerged unprompted in digital evolution (Tierra's parasites → hyper-parasites → social hosts → cheaters) when organisms can interact through shared resources and each other's "bodies", and real ecology supplies concrete, implementable mechanisms for relationships that *develop* — reputation/image scoring, partner switching and punishment, learned search images, and frequency-dependent signals (mimicry). Legibility is a separate problem that shipped games solve with selective memory plus curation (Nemesis), pacing managers (RimWorld), and browsable history (Dwarf Fortress Legends, Species' web-of-life view).

### Cited Findings
- Tierra (1991): with no parasite code written, an ecology of parasites, immune hosts, immunity-evading parasites, hyper-parasites (which subverted parasites for their own reproduction and drove them toward extinction), cooperating "social" hosts and cheating hyper-hyper-parasites emerged — [Univ. of Delaware Messenger, 1992](https://www1.udel.edu/PR/Messenger/92/2/16.html); [Ray, Tierra paper](https://faculty.cc.gatech.edu/~turk/bio_sim/articles/tierra_thomas_ray.pdf)
- Polyworld (1994): Yaeger wrote that "predation, mimicry, sexual reproduction, and even communication are all supported in a straightforward fashion", with complex survival strategies arising as emergent phenomena; agents' primitives were move, turn, eat, mate, attack and light (a visible signal) — [Yaeger, ALife III paper](https://shinyverse.org/larryy/Yaeger.ALife3.pdf)
- Avida host–parasite coevolution produces significantly *nested* interaction networks compared with random ones, and parasites retain a genetic record of past host states — [Zaman dissertation, MSU 2014](https://d.lib.msu.edu/etd/2442); [Zaman et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4267771)
- Cross-feeding as an origin of dependence: in *E. coli*, adaptive radiation can occur because metabolites produced by some genotypes serve as resources for others — [Chow et al. 2004](https://www.rifters.com/real/articles/Science_DigitalOrganisms.pdf)
- Cleaning symbiosis runs on reputation: client fish that could eavesdrop spent more time next to cleaners they had seen cooperating than next to cleaners of unknown cooperativeness (image scoring), and cleaners fed more cooperatively when being observed. The underlying conflict is that cleaners prefer client mucus/tissue to parasites; clients also control cheating by partner switching and aggressive chasing (punishment) — [Bshary & Grutter, Nature 2006](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1038/nature04755); [PMC2293952](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2293952)
- Juvenile cleaner fish can socially learn, by observing adults, both to behave more cooperatively and to prefer clients that tolerate cheating (2020) — [summary record](https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/works/4VqPxZk7)
- Mimicry is a signal economy with opposite frequency dependence: a harmless Batesian mimic is protected only while rare relative to its model (negative frequency dependence), whereas Müllerian co-mimics that are all genuinely defended benefit the more common the shared warning signal is (positive frequency dependence); the two form a spectrum — [Papilio polytes study, PMC6540699](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6540699/); [Wikipedia: Müllerian mimicry](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mullerian_mimicry); [Wikipedia: Frequency-dependent selection](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frequency-dependent_selection)
- Keystone predation: when Paine removed the sea star *Pisaster* from an intertidal site (1966), mussels took over and species count in the plot fell from 15 to 8 within about a year — [Nature Scitable: Keystone species](https://www.nature.com/scitable/knowledge/library/keystone-species-15786127). A 2016 re-analysis complicates this: the mussels are a *foundation species* whose beds house 300+ associated species, so removal can raise community-wide diversity depending on what is counted — [Lafferty & Suchanek 2016 (USGS record)](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70175674)
- Predator learning generates persistent pairwise dynamics: frequency-dependent predation driven by predator learning gave long-lasting prey coexistence with periodic oscillations — [study summary](https://peeref.com/works/15114414)
- Shadow of Mordor's Nemesis system (2014) was built around the question "How can we make narrative out of the gameplay?" (design director Michael de Plater); its first version simply had enemies remember meeting the player and taunt accordingly; the shipped system added named enemies who rise in rank after surviving encounters, and it was inspired by real-world sports rivalries — [MCV on de Plater's DICE talk](https://mcvuk.com/development-news/monoliths-nemesis-system-inspired-by-real-world-sports/); [inkl: "In Shadow of Mordor, revenge gets personal"](https://www.inkl.com/news/in-shadow-of-mordor-revenge-gets-personal)
- Nemesis memory is selective — it keeps consequences that matter for the next encounter (who won, who escaped, how status changed) — and Monolith hand-curated the procedural output, removing dull or bad outcomes; Chris Hoge's GDC 2018 talk "Helping Players Hate (or Love) Their Nemesis" focused on long-term player–orc relationships. An earlier prototype had significantly more layers than shipped — [GDC Vault session](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1025150/contactUs); [Game Informer on Shadow of War](https://gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2017/09/22/the-unchained-chaos-of-middle-earth-shadow-of-war); [Game Informer, 2015](https://www.gameinformer.com/b/news/archive/2015/01/20/shadow-of-mordor-s-nemesis-system-was-once-a-bit-more-complicated.aspx)
- RimWorld is framed by Tynan Sylvester as a "story generator"; he has said players extract more story meaning from an event than is represented in the mechanics. Its AI storytellers manage pacing — Cassandra follows rising/falling tension, Phoebe is laid-back, Randy forsakes pacing — and try not to over-punish struggling players. Sylvester was inspired more by player-written Dwarf Fortress stories (Boatmurdered, Gemclod) than by DF's interface — [Game Developer: how RimWorld fleshes out the Dwarf Fortress formula](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/how-i-rimworld-i-fleshes-out-the-i-dwarf-fortress-i-formula); [Rock Paper Shotgun, 2016](https://www.rockpapershotgun.com/2016/08/12/how-rimworld-generates-great-stories); [Wikipedia: RimWorld](https://Www.wikipedia.org/wiki/RimWorld)
- Dwarf Fortress Legends mode lets players browse generated history: maps, civilisation histories and any tracked figure; only a small percentage of the population is tracked explicitly as "historical figures"; the full history can be exported as XML (which can exceed a gigabyte) — [DF wiki: Legends](https://dwarffortresswiki.org/legends); [DF wiki: Historical figure](https://dwarffortresswiki.org/index.php/Historical_figure); [DF wiki: XML dump](https://dwarffortresswiki.org/index.php/XML_dump)
- Species: ALRE ships a clade diagram, a satellite map of species ranges and a "web of life" view; The Bibites ships lineage trees — [Steam: Species ALRE](https://store.steampowered.com/app/774541?l=italian); [The Bibites](https://thebibites.itch.io/the-bibites)
- Player-level diplomacy precedent: Neptune's Pride is pitched as logging in a few times a day to command your empire "and conspire with the other players" — [Iron Helmet Games](https://ironhelmet.com/)

### Inferences
- **A minimal mechanism set that lets relationships form over time (proposal):**
  1. *Recognisable identity:* each species broadcasts a designer-chosen signal (colour/pattern/scent channel) that other species' brains can take as input. This single feature enables search images, avoidance, mimicry (a cheap "copy signal" part gives Batesian/Müllerian dynamics for free) and warning coloration.
  2. *Per-species learned valence:* each species (or each individual, inherited with decay) keeps a small table "signal X → good/bad for me" updated from outcomes (ate it, was hurt near it, gained energy near it). This is the image-scoring/partner-choice mechanism from cleaner fish and the selective memory of Nemesis.
  3. *Tradable services:* at least two things one species can do for another besides being eaten — e.g. parasite removal (cleaning), waste-as-food (cross-feeding/detritus), alarm calls, seed/egg dispersal — plus the temptation to cheat (cleaner fish), so mutualisms are unstable enough to produce betrayals.
- **Derive a relationship ledger automatically.** For each ordered species pair, log energy flow and survival effect over a window; classify by sign pair (+/− predation or parasitism, +/+ mutualism, +/0 commensalism, −/− competition) and track *changes* ("your grazers and Ana's cleaner-shrimp went from commensal to mutualist on day 3"). The classification is standard ecology; using it as a UI ledger is my proposal.
- **Legibility recipe from the game precedents:** track a small cast of "notable individuals" and species-pair histories (DF's historical-figure subset), keep only consequential events (Nemesis), curate out dull ones, pace the reveals (RimWorld), and present a food-web graph whose edges thicken/change colour over time (Species' web-of-life). Sports-style framing ("rivalry", "upset", "streak") worked for Nemesis and fits a friend group.
- **Player diplomacy is an additional layer, not a substitute.** With 5–20 friends, out-of-game chat will carry negotiation ("don't eat my grazers and I'll leave your nest sites alone"); the game only needs to make pacts *expressible in behaviour* (a "do not attack signal X" rule) and their breaches *visible* in the recap.
- **Keystone/foundation roles are worth surfacing as honours** ("removing species A would have collapsed 3 others"), computed by counterfactual batch sims; the Paine/Lafferty debate is a caution that "importance" depends on what you count.

### Gaps
- No retrieved precedent for automatically generated "nature documentary" recaps or LLM-narrated ecosystem summaries; the recap ideas here are extrapolations from Nemesis/RimWorld/DF.
- No sources retrieved on Neptune's Pride/Subterfuge/Diplomacy diplomacy mechanics in detail, or on relationship systems in The Bibites/Thrive beyond lineage and food-web views.
- Commensalism, trophic levels and competition types were not separately sourced (textbook material); mimicry and cleaning-symbiosis sources are abstracts/secondary summaries.
- No evidence on whether players actually perceive emergent mutualisms without heavy UI support; Lively et al.'s caution about statistically invisible mechanisms suggests they may not.

## 6. Session and social structure: asynchronous play for a friend group on phones, notifications, drop-outs, late joiners, seasons

### Takeaway
The two closest shipped precedents, Neptune's Pride and Subterfuge, both converge on "a few short check-ins a day over one to several weeks", and Subterfuge's designers explicitly treated *minimising required check-ins* and respecting players' time as design pillars — yet even so a critic titled a piece "my favorite game of 2015, but I never want to play it again", a warning about social-pressure burnout. Leavers and AFK players are a documented sore point in Neptune's Pride; an autonomous-species game avoids most of that because the world does not depend on any one player acting.

### Cited Findings
- Neptune's Pride: "real-time but played over several weeks. You can log in at any time of the day to check the progress of your ships, view the results of battles, and issue new orders"; players log in "a few times a day"; games take 2–6 weeks — [NP help: intro](https://np.ironhelmet.com/help/intro); [Iron Helmet Games](https://ironhelmet.com/); [Game Developer interview](https://gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/interview-space-lions-iron-helmet-talks-i-neptune-s-pride-i-)
- Neptune's Pride's later version added mobile play and a turn-based mode; a player notes that real-time games effectively run like turn-based ones because there are no partial ticks; community games are advertised with e.g. 48-hour turn deadlines — [Iron Helmet forum: nostalgia thread](https://forum.ironhelmet.com/t/nostalgia-for-the-original-np/6487); [Iron Helmet forum: turn-based game listing](https://forum.ironhelmet.com/t/4d-chess-6-turn-based-48h-deadlines-3-day-jumps/6395)
- Neptune's Pride inactivity rule: once a game is running and the player has logged in at least once, they must log in every 71 ticks or they are marked AFK; an AI may then act for them (the developer says it should leave existing orders alone on the first missed turn; a player reported otherwise) — [forum: what counts as logged in for AFK](https://forum.ironhelmet.com/t/what-counts-as-logged-in-for-afk/3741); [forum: AI hijacking](https://forum.ironhelmet.com/t/ai-hijacking/5977)
- Leavers are an acknowledged problem: a thread titled "Leavers ruin big room games. Someone is going to get a massive unfair advantage" describes quitters' fleets sitting idle to a neighbour's benefit; letting new players take over quit/AFK seats is a *suggested* (not existing) feature, with a proposed restriction to games where no one has reached half the victory condition — [forum: leavers](https://forum.ironhelmet.com/t/leavers-ruin-big-room-games-someone-is-going-to-get-a-massive-unfair-advantage-any-solutions-to-this/8301); [forum: replacing AIs](https://forum.ironhelmet.com/t/replacing-ais/3678)
- Subterfuge (2015): designers wanted "to keep the intensity and depth, but temper it and control it so that it's a game that we can play for a few minutes here and there over the course of a week or two"; an interview is headlined "We wanted to minimise the number of times per day a player has to check in"; a session lasts minutes while a game lasts a week or more — [MCV interview](https://www.mcvuk.com/we-wanted-to-minimise-the-number-of-times-per-day-a-player-has-to-check-in/); [Kill Screen](https://www.killscreen.com/subterfuge-strategy-game-parents-and-busy-people/); [subterfuge-game.com](https://subterfuge-game.com/)
- Noel Llopis: "one of our design pillars in the development of Subterfuge is respecting players' time", in a post covering quality-of-life choices including push notifications — [Game Developer: Quality of life in Subterfuge](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/quality-of-life-in-subterfuge)
- Counter-evidence on healthiness: a Game Developer piece is titled "Subterfuge was my favorite game of 2015, but I never want to play it again" (title-level evidence of burnout); another critic found the long match made it feel like a persistent part of life but player-to-player interaction was a weak point for long-term play — [Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/subterfuge-was-my-favorite-game-of-2015-but-i-never-want-to-play-it-again); [AV Club: The Long Game](https://www.avclub.com/the-long-game-subterfuge-formalism-and-interactivi)
- Wordle: Josh Wardle said one puzzle per day creates scarcity that leaves players wanting more and keeps play to about three minutes a day; everyone getting the same puzzle created the shared social ritual he calls a defining factor; the spoiler-free emoji-grid share was invented by a player and then built in; no app, account or paywall — just a link — [Wikipedia: Wordle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wordle); [NPR, Jan 2022](https://www.npr.org/2022/01/14/1072980440/a-simple-word-game-is-the-newest-social-media-phenomenon)
- Seasons as the standard answer to late-joiner/veteran gaps in persistent worlds: PoE (~13 weeks, fresh economy), Screeps (~2 months, fresh world, varied rules), Rust (monthly forced map wipe) — see Section 1 — [PoE wiki](https://www.poewiki.net/wiki/League); [ScreepsPlus wiki](https://wiki.screepspl.us/Subscription); [runonflux](https://rust.runonflux.com/wipe-schedule)

### Inferences
- **Structural advantage of this design:** species act autonomously, so a player who disappears for three days harms no one and loses little — unlike Neptune's Pride, where an absent player distorts the map. Drop-outs simply stop releasing; their last species lives out its natural span and becomes part of the ecosystem's history. No AI takeover is needed.
- **Late joiners:** per-release scoring (Section 1) plus group-wide unlock catalogue (Section 3) means a newcomer can release a competitive species on day one. A season boundary every ~4–8 weeks (shorter than PoE/Screeps because the group is small and species live under a week, so a season still holds ~5–10 generations) gives a natural "everyone restart" moment and a place to change world rules.
- **Check-in cadence:** design for *one* satisfying daily visit with optional extra glances, not for required multiple check-ins. Subterfuge's stated goal and its critic's burnout both point the same way: anything that makes more frequent checking *advantageous* will be felt as pressure in a friend group.
- **Notifications (proposal):** default to one daily digest at a player-chosen time; opt-in event pings only for rare, consequential events (first birth, first predator contact, population below 10%, extinction, a friend's species interacting with yours); quiet hours; never notify to announce a penalty for absence.
- **Shared ritual and shareability:** Wordle's lesson is same-thing-for-everyone plus a compact, spoiler-free share object. Candidates: a daily world headline everyone sees ("Day 12: the long night"), and a one-image "species card" (population sparkline, age, top relationship) pasteable into the group chat.
- **Spectating:** a read-only world view and other players' species pages cost little and feed the social loop; hiding the *brain* of living species until extinction preserves design secrecy while still giving a post-mortem reveal.

## 7. World/ecosystem simulation design: minimal ingredients for rich niches, and avoiding collapse or monoculture

### Takeaway
Artificial-life results point to a short list of diversity-preserving ingredients: **several distinct limiting resources at intermediate (not maximal) productivity**, **coevolving enemies**, some spatial structure, and temporal fluctuation — plus explicit protection against the demographic-noise extinction cascades that small populations suffer. Over-enrichment is a documented route to boom–bust collapse.

### Cited Findings
- Avida (Chow et al., Science 2004): a diverse pool of limiting resources was *sufficient* to produce and maintain phenotypically and phylogenetically distinct groups, even in a spatially homogeneous environment, through frequency-dependent selection; species richness peaked at **intermediate productivity**. Earlier Avida work had used effectively unlimited resources (logic-task rewards), which does not produce this coexistence — [Chow et al. 2004 (full text)](https://www.rifters.com/real/articles/Science_DigitalOrganisms.pdf); [abstract](https://scilit.com/publications/7c2bb288f8c72cd60844b1823d7c480d); [Chow thesis, Caltech 2005](https://thesis.library.caltech.edu/2478/1/thesis.pdf)
- By contrast, in the bacterium *Pseudomonas fluorescens* spatial heterogeneity was necessary for adaptive radiation, and in *E. coli* radiation arose via cross-feeding on metabolites — [Chow et al. 2004](https://www.rifters.com/real/articles/Science_DigitalOrganisms.pdf)
- Parasites/enemies maintain and even generate diversity and complexity in digital worlds (Avida), and whole parasite ecologies can emerge when organisms can use each other's resources (Tierra) — [Zaman et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4267771); [BEACON blog](https://beacon-center.org/?p=676); [Ray, Tierra](https://faculty.cc.gatech.edu/~turk/bio_sim/articles/tierra_thomas_ray.pdf)
- Small-population noise is the main threat to a kill-the-winner world: demographic stochasticity collapsed coexistence through extinction cascades unless predators and prey could coevolve — [Xue & Goldenfeld 2017](https://link.aps.org/accepted/10.1103/PhysRevLett.119.268101)
- Enrichment destabilises: raising carrying capacity pushes predator–prey systems into large oscillations with near-zero troughs — [Wikipedia: Paradox of enrichment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradox_of_enrichment)
- Temporal fluctuation supports coexistence through the storage effect and relative nonlinearity (Hutchinson's plankton argument), though the simple "intermediate disturbance" prediction is poorly supported empirically — [MCT review, arXiv 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.07926v1.pdf); [Fox 2013](https://typeset.io/pdf/the-intermediate-disturbance-hypothesis-should-be-abandoned-2hdlp3utm0.pdf)
- A keystone predator can prevent a superior competitor from monopolising space (Paine 1966), but a "monopolist" can itself be habitat for hundreds of species (Lafferty & Suchanek 2016) — [Scitable](https://www.nature.com/scitable/knowledge/library/keystone-species-15786127); [USGS record](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70175674)
- Polyworld's world was minimal — a flat ground plane with randomly grown food, agents with colour vision and seven primitive behaviours — yet produced distinct behavioural "species" (e.g. "frenetic joggers" that ran straight ahead always trying to eat and mate), showing that degenerate simple strategies are the default winners in a featureless world — [Yaeger, ALife III paper](https://shinyverse.org/larryy/Yaeger.ALife3.pdf); [Ars Electronica 1993](https://webarchive.ars.electronica.art/en/archives/festival_archive/festival_documentations/1993/yaege.html)
- Open-ended evolution — how to keep generating novelty indefinitely in an artificial system — is still described as an open question in artificial life — [Tierra overview (mirror)](https://cavac.at/cavacopedia/tierra_%28computer_simulation%29) (low-quality mirror; treat as weak support)
- Practical data point: The Bibites prunes old, irrelevant species from history to control data size; Species: ALRE gives players global catastrophes (ice age, flood) as disturbance tools — [Bibites 0.6.0 devlog](https://thebibites.itch.io/the-bibites/devlog/809631/the-bibites-060-organs-and-science); [Steam: Species ALRE](https://store.steampowered.com/app/774541?l=italian)

### Inferences
- **Minimal niche-rich world (proposal derived from the above):**
  - *Resources (3–4, each depletable and separately renewing):* light-fed plants, detritus/carrion (produced by deaths — closes the loop and creates a decomposer niche), a patchy mineral/water resource, and living prey. Chow et al. imply the number of *limiting* resources bounds the number of stable consumer types, so each added resource type is worth more than many added parts.
  - *Space (2–3 biomes along one gradient, e.g. wet→dry or shallow→deep) with refuges:* spatial structure was required for radiation in some real systems and gives prey hiding places that damp predator–prey crashes.
  - *Time:* day/night (favours sensing/activity-pattern trade-offs) and a season cycle whose period is of the same order as the target species lifespan (so most species meet one seasonal turn); announce forecasts.
  - *Hazards (1–2 abiotic):* cold/heat or drought; plus rare player-visible events (flood, bloom) as "intermediate" disturbance — keep modest since evidence for disturbance-peaked diversity is weak.
  - *Enemies:* the specialist-enemy layer from Section 1, which doubles as the anti-monoculture system.
- **Run the world as a chemostat, not a feast:** fixed resource *influx* per tick set to intermediate productivity, auto-scaled by number of active species, rather than large standing stocks; this is the direct lesson of both the Avida productivity result and the paradox of enrichment.
- **Guard against small-number collapse:** with only 5–20 player species, add a background of NPC "native" species to fill the food web and absorb noise, allow low-rate re-immigration from a seed bank/eggs for the first day or two of a species' life (protects against unlucky launch, not against decline), and keep the enemy layer adaptive (coevolving) as Xue & Goldenfeld suggest.
- **Design against the "frenetic jogger":** if the simplest eat-and-breed strategy wins, brains are pointless. Patchy, seasonal and defended resources (things that require sensing, timing or cooperation to exploit) are what make brain budget pay.
- **Server cost:** adopt Bibites-style pruning (aggregate dead lineages into summary stats + a few notable individuals) so 24/7 history stays small.

### Gaps
- Lenia and ALIEN were not sourced (a combined search returned only Karl Sims results); nothing here should be attributed to them.
- Polyworld's energy/metabolic cost model and any quantitative Polyworld diversity results were not retrieved.
- No source gives a validated "minimal set" of biomes/resources for a game; the list above is inference from ALife and ecology findings.
- No retrieved information on tick rates, world sizes or population counts that are feasible for a hobby-scale 24/7 server.

## 8. Scope addition — "Tamagotchi × Spore × neural-design": the ~15-minute daily check-in, light-touch intervention, mid-life edits, the "while you were away" recap, and a weekly design session

### Takeaway
Virtual-pet attachment comes from *behaviour and felt responsibility*, not graphics, but the original Tamagotchi's neglect-means-death rule caused enough distress that later models added a pause; the stronger precedents for this game are the ones that make absence harmless or even productive (Neko Atsume) and make *how you treated the creature* change what it becomes rather than whether it survives (Digimon V-Pet). For edits, Spore and Thrive both restrict redesign to generation boundaries and Thrive pairs it with a prediction panel — a direct template for "small daily evolution, big weekly redesign". **This section is only partly sourced: the search budget ran out, so god-game, idle-game, Animal Crossing, Nintendogs/Neopets/Finch/Pikmin Bloom/Viridi and fantasy-sports precedents are in Gaps as unverified recollection.**

### Cited Findings

#### Care loops, attachment, guilt and absence
- The "Tamagotchi effect" is the development of emotional attachment to machines, robots or software agents. Commentators locate the emotional weight "not from graphics or narrative, but from behavior" (Paola Antonelli, MoMA) and in a sense of responsibility; Bandai attributed the product's success to the human nurturing instinct; one cited explanation is the customisation/personalisation a virtual pet allows — [Wikipedia: Tamagotchi effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamagotchi_effect)
- Neglect was punished hard in the original: an unattended pet would "die" within half a day, causing emotional distress and forcing a device reset; newer models added a pause feature in response; people held funerals, and UK pet cemeteries set aside sections for digital pets. The same article reports that people with anxious-ambivalent attachment are most likely to form such attachments — [Wikipedia: Tamagotchi effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamagotchi_effect)
- Neko Atsume (2014): developer Yutaka Takazaki's goal was a game even children could enjoy "without a significant investment of skill or time"; play is placing objects and waiting; a critic describes the core conceit as nothing happening while you watch — the interesting things occur in your absence, "absence makes the heart grow fonder". Takazaki has said he does not know why it became so popular — [Wikipedia: Neko Atsume](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neko_Atsume); [Ludus Novus: "The Feline Grace of Neko Atsume", 2015](https://ludusnovus.net/2015/07/30/the-feline-grace-of-neko-atsume)
- Digimon V-Pet (Digital Monster Ver. 1, 1997): the adult form is chosen by how the creature was treated — e.g. one branch requires 0–3 "care mistakes" and 32+ training sessions, another 4+ care mistakes with 5–15 trainings, 3+ overfeeds and 0–4 sleep disturbances; some forms are fallbacks reached when no other requirement is met; counters (care mistakes, training, injuries, battles) reset at each evolution, and if requirements are unmet when the timer expires the check simply repeats later. Fan-compiled charts; thresholds vary by version — [Wikimon: Digital Monster Ver. 1](https://wikimon.net/Digital_Monster_Ver._1); [With the Will V-Pet evolution guide](https://lcd.withthewill.net/VpetEvo1); [Humulos V-Pet guide](https://humulos.com/digimon/dmx/3)
- Scarcity as a healthy limiter: Wordle's one-per-day design was intended to leave players wanting more and to take ~3 minutes a day — [Wikipedia: Wordle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wordle)
- Subterfuge's designers made "respecting players' time" a pillar and aimed to minimise required check-ins per day — [Game Developer: Quality of life in Subterfuge](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/quality-of-life-in-subterfuge); [MCV](https://www.mcvuk.com/we-wanted-to-minimise-the-number-of-times-per-day-a-player-has-to-check-in/)

#### Light-touch intervention and mid-life edits on a living species
- Spore's creature stage ties editing to reproduction: parts found in the world are added to the editor's pool for use at the next mating call, so redesign happens at a generation boundary rather than on the living individual — [GameSpot hands-on](https://gamespot.com/articles/spore-exclusive-hands-on-from-cell-to-civilization/1100-6195809/)
- Thrive likewise has the player evolve their species manually in an editor between generations while the simulation ("auto-evo") evolves everything else; it added an in-editor prediction panel showing how auto-evo rates the edited species, and exposes how strongly the simulation's population verdict applies to the player (raised from 20% to 50% in January 2025, configurable) — [Thrive Devblog #20](https://revolutionarygamesstudio.com/devblog-20-lets-try-to-solve-evolution/); [Thrive patch 0.5.6.1](https://itch.io/devlog/323660/patch-0561.amp); [Thrive progress update 2025-01-25](https://revolutionarygamesstudio.com/progress-update-01-25-2025/)
- Species: ALRE offers only indirect god-tools on an autonomous evolving world: a nursery (isolated sandbox for the gene pool), rovers that let the player choose which creatures survive, and global catastrophes — [Steam: Species ALRE](https://store.steampowered.com/app/774541?l=italian)
- Avida shows a principled in-fiction reason for small edits to matter: hosts that acquire a new function escape parasites that had specialised on the old phenotype — [Zaman et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4267771)

#### What makes a "what happened while I was away" recap worth opening
- Nemesis: keep only consequential memories (who won, who escaped, whose status changed), attach them to named individuals, and curate out dull outcomes — [GDC Vault: Helping Players Hate (or Love) Their Nemesis](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1025150/contactUs); [Game Informer](https://gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2017/09/22/the-unchained-chaos-of-middle-earth-shadow-of-war)
- RimWorld: players read more story into events than the mechanics contain, and a storyteller shapes pacing into rising and falling tension — [Rock Paper Shotgun, 2016](https://www.rockpapershotgun.com/2016/08/12/how-rimworld-generates-great-stories); [Wikipedia: RimWorld](https://Www.wikipedia.org/wiki/RimWorld)
- Dwarf Fortress: a browsable history built around a *small tracked subset* of notable figures — [DF wiki: Historical figure](https://dwarffortresswiki.org/index.php/Historical_figure)
- Wordle: a compact, spoiler-free, shareable result object drove the social loop, and it was a player invention the developer adopted — [Wikipedia: Wordle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wordle)
- ALife-game analysis views usable as recap components: lineage trees (The Bibites); clade diagram, satellite range map, web-of-life (Species) — [The Bibites](https://thebibites.itch.io/the-bibites); [Steam: Species ALRE](https://store.steampowered.com/app/774541?l=italian)

#### Weekly long session alternating with short daily maintenance
- Neptune's Pride and Subterfuge establish the rhythm of minutes-long daily sessions inside a multi-day or multi-week arc — [NP help](https://np.ironhelmet.com/help/intro); [subterfuge-game.com](https://subterfuge-game.com/)
- League/season starts act as a periodic "big session" where everyone rebuilds from scratch (PoE ~13 weeks; Screeps ~2 months with new rules) — [PoE wiki](https://www.poewiki.net/wiki/League); [ScreepsPlus wiki](https://wiki.screepspl.us/Subscription)
- Between-release practice content exists as a pattern: Cell Lab's challenges are self-contained design puzzles that unlock new cell types — [namu.moe: Cell Lab](https://namu.moe/w/%EC%85%80%EB%9E%A9)

### Inferences
- **Reframe "care" for an autonomous species.** The species never *needs* the player, so absence is never punished (the Neko Atsume stance, and the lesson of Tamagotchi's pause feature). What the player's attention buys is *influence and knowledge*: observations, small evolutions, and a better next design. Death is expected within days and is the trigger for the creative session and for unlocks (Section 3), not a failure caused by neglect — which removes the guilt mechanic while keeping the responsibility-and-behaviour source of attachment.
- **Borrow Digimon's branching, not Tamagotchi's dying.** "How it was treated" can select between *variants* (e.g. a species that was nudged often toward the wetland drifts amphibious; one left alone becomes hardier) with fallback forms for hands-off players, so both attentive and absent styles produce a distinct, legitimate outcome.
- **A 15-minute session budget (proposal):** ~3 min recap → ~4 min live observation (follow one individual, tap to see which neurons/rules fired — the neural-design payoff) → ~3 min one or two bounded actions → ~5 min notebook/sandbox for the next design. Every step is skippable; the recap alone should be a complete 60-second session.
- **Bounded daily actions that do not become micro-management:** (a) one *evolution point* per day — a single parameter tweak or one part/neuron swap; (b) one *nudge* with a ~24 h cooldown — drop a marker pheromone, open/close a nest site, sound an alarm — acting on the *environment or signals*, never puppeteering individuals; (c) name/tag a notable individual. Unused allowances bank to a small maximum (2–3) so a missed day costs nothing but hoarding is pointless. The precedent pattern (Species' rovers/catastrophes, Spore/Thrive generation-gated editing) supports "indirect and rate-limited" as the shape; the specific numbers are mine.
- **Mid-life edit policies and their trade-offs:**
  - *Locked at release:* strongest "run" integrity and simplest scoring; daily session is observation-only, which risks thin engagement.
  - *Edits apply to newborns only* (Spore/Thrive pattern): old and new morphs coexist and selection arbitrates — legible, self-limiting, and produces a visible lineage split for the recap. Recommended baseline.
  - *Daily allowance:* caps total drift over a 5-day life to ~5 small changes, so the launch design still dominates the outcome.
  - *Edits cost budget:* require a "plasticity reserve" set aside at design time (unspent budget = future edits), making adaptability a costed trait and rewarding foresight.
  - *Edits reset enemy adaptation only partially and with diminishing returns* (Avida escape logic): each tweak buys hours, not days, so the 3–7-day envelope holds.
  - *Never retroactive and never free of the cap:* an edit must keep the design within the fixed budget, or the cap stops meaning anything.
- **Recap design (proposal from the cited patterns):** 3–5 headlines ranked by consequence, each tied to a named individual or species pair; one population sparkline with annotated turning points; one relationship change ("first time eaten by…", "now cleaning for…"); a forecast (season change, rising parasite load); and exactly one decision prompt ("spend today's evolution point?"). Add a share card for the group chat. Curate aggressively — Nemesis's lesson is that dull events must be cut, and RimWorld's is that players will supply the meaning.
- **Weekly cadence with variable death timing:** let the long session be *player-pulled*. Provide a persistent design notebook and a "successor in incubation" slot that can be worked on during the current species' life (fed by daily observations), a sandbox that tests the draft against a snapshot of today's ecosystem with a Thrive-style prediction readout, and no penalty for taking a day or two after extinction before releasing (per-release scoring makes delay neutral). Between releases, Cell-Lab-style challenge puzzles give something to do and can gate unlocks.
- **Risk to watch:** if daily evolution points are strong, the optimal play becomes "launch anything, then patch daily", which devalues the weekly design session; if they are weak, the daily session is hollow. The plasticity-reserve rule is the cleanest way to let the player choose their own point on that spectrum.
