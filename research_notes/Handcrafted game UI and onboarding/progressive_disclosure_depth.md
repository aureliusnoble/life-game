## 1. Progressive disclosure as a principle (NN/G, Apple, Material, game UX writers), its costs, and progressive versus staged disclosure

### Takeaway
Progressive disclosure means showing a small set of the most important options first and the rest only on request; it is credited with better learnability, efficiency and error rates, but only when the split between first and second layer is right and the route to the second layer is obvious, and it degrades past about two levels. Staged disclosure is a different thing (a linear sequence everyone walks through), and games add a third mechanism, unlock-gating, whose main documented cost is making capable players wait.

### Cited Findings
- Nielsen (NN/G, December 2006, desktop/web era) defines progressive disclosure as deferring advanced or rarely used features to a secondary screen, which makes applications easier to learn and less error-prone. [snippet + recall] — [NN/G: Progressive Disclosure](https://nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/)
- The same article credits the technique with improving three of usability's components: learnability, efficiency of use and error rate. Showing something on the initial display tells users it is important; hiding advanced settings keeps novices from making mistakes with them. [snippet] — [NN/G: Progressive Disclosure](https://nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/)
- NN/G names two things a designer must get right: the split between initial and secondary features, and making it obvious how to get from the first level to the second (simple mechanics, a label that sets expectations). [snippet for the split; recall for the second] — [NN/G: Progressive Disclosure](https://nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/)
- NN/G warns that designs going beyond two disclosure levels typically have low usability because users get lost moving between levels; if three or more levels seem necessary, simplify the design. [recall] — [NN/G: Progressive Disclosure](https://nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/)
- Staged disclosure, per NN/G, is a variant where the user moves through a sequence of steps and sees a subset of features at each (the wizard is the typical example). It works best when steps have little interaction with each other and becomes a problem when users must go back and forth between interdependent steps. [snippet] — [NN/G: Progressive Disclosure](https://nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/)
- The distinction in one line, as secondary summaries put it: in progressive disclosure most people never reach the second layer; in staged disclosure everyone goes through every step. One concept site lists "staged disclosure" as an alias of progressive disclosure, which conflicts with NN/G; NN/G's wording should be preferred. [snippet] — [The Decision Lab](https://thedecisionlab.com/reference-guide/design/progressive-disclosure); [dsebastien concepts](https://concepts.dsebastien.net/concept/progressive-disclosure/)
- Jakob Nielsen has a recent long article, "Progressive Disclosure: From Training Wheels to Week-Long AI Agents" (date not shown in the snippet). It states that Apple's 1992 Macintosh Human Interface Guidelines had a section on progressive disclosure and that Apple still ships a control family named for the idea (the disclosure triangle and relatives). Not checked against Apple's own document. [snippet] — [UX Tigers](https://uxtigers.com/post/progressive-disclosure); [Substack copy](https://jakobnielsenphd.substack.com/p/progressive-disclosure)
- The "training wheels" in that title refers to Carroll and Carrasco's 1984 study (Communications of the ACM): a word processor with advanced functions blocked for new users. As I recall the result, novices on the blocked version got started faster, spent less time recovering from errors, and did better on a later comprehension test. This is the oldest controlled evidence for the idea. [recall; the Nielsen article is the nearest surfaced source, the study itself was not retrieved] — [UX Tigers](https://uxtigers.com/post/progressive-disclosure)
- Apple's current HIG onboarding page (touch-first platforms) advises a brief, enjoyable onboarding that does not require people to memorise a lot of information, and says people do not need to learn the system or device during onboarding. Several third-party pages claim Apple recommends at most three onboarding screens; the Apple page as surfaced does not state a number, so treat that figure as unverified. [snippet] — [Apple HIG: Onboarding](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/onboarding)
- Apple's WWDC22 session on SwiftUI API design describes progressive disclosure as making the complexity of the call site grow with the complexity of the use case. It is about APIs, not screens, but it is the cleanest statement of the principle for a sentence-shaped editor: the simple case should be short, and each added capability should add one visible piece. [snippet] — [WWDC22 session 10059](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2022/10059/?time=276)
- Celia Hodent's GDC 2016 talk "The Gamer's Brain, Part 2: UX of Onboarding and Player Engagement" (57 minutes, Epic Games/Fortnite era) is organised as Discovery (remove barriers), Learning (active tutorials), Immersion (motivation). [snippet] — [Class Central listing](https://classcentral.com/course/youtube-the-gamer-s-brain-part-2-ux-of-onboarding-and-player-engagement-158058)
- From the book of the same name (2017), as I recall it: Hodent recommends an explicit onboarding plan (list everything a player must learn, rate each item's importance and difficulty, then decide when and how each is taught), teaching by doing in a meaningful context rather than by text, and spreading lessons over time to respect limited attention and working memory. The phrase "learning by doing" was not confirmed by the search. [recall] — [Class Central listing](https://classcentral.com/course/youtube-the-gamer-s-brain-part-2-ux-of-onboarding-and-player-engagement-158058)
- George Fan (GDC 2012) gives the motivational reason to stage: "When I first start playing, I only have a certain willingness to learn things. But as I play it and become invested in the game, I have more of a willingness to learn." [snippet] — [Game Developer: 10 tutorial tips from PvZ creator George Fan](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/gdc-2012-10-tutorial-tips-from-i-plants-vs-zombies-i-creator-george-fan)
- Documented costs, from games in this session:
  - Hidden depth judged worse: on console, Crusader Kings III's nested tooltips sit "behind a specific tooltip mode", and a reviewer notes the PC version lets you open multiple layers easily while console does not (console, 2022). [snippet] — [TheSixthAxis review](https://thesixthaxis.com/2022/03/29/crusader-kings-3-console-review)
  - Gating that starves the early game: one player wrote they did not like Final Fantasy XII at all until around hour 15, when more than the most basic gambits became available (console, 2006 game). [snippet; which of two surfaced blogs carries the line is unclear] — [GNG Games blog, 2015](https://gnggames.blogspot.com/2015/02/final-fantasy-xii-dragon-age-origins.html); [RPG Tourist blog, 2022](https://rpgtourist.blogspot.com/2022/09/exploring-tactical-systems-in-final.html)
  - Gating that makes experts wait: Super Mario Maker's nine-day tool unlock drew a negative community reaction and was patched at launch (console with touch screen, 2015; details in section 5). [snippet] — [GamesRadar](https://gamesradar.com/super-mario-maker-update-unlocks)

### Inferences
- Three mechanisms are being lumped together and should be kept apart in the design: (a) progressive disclosure proper, where the feature exists and is one obvious tap away; (b) staged disclosure, a fixed sequence everyone passes through, such as a first-creature walkthrough; (c) unlock-gating, where the feature does not exist yet for this player. NN/G's evidence covers (a) and (b). The costs found in games (Mario Maker, FFXII) are costs of (c).
- For a sentence "notice -> do" with optional strength and up to two conditions, the SwiftUI formulation fits directly: the bare sentence is the first layer; strength and conditions are second-layer pieces that appear only when asked for. Moods and signals are better treated as unlocks than as a third and fourth disclosure layer, because NN/G's two-level limit argues against sentence -> detail -> sub-detail -> sub-sub-detail.
- NN/G's point that what is shown first is read as important cuts both ways: the first screen of the editor teaches players what the game is about. If strength sliders are on the first screen, players will assume tuning numbers is the game.
- No source in this session measured the cost to experts of extra taps. The CK3 lock-mode design (timer by default, switchable to explicit action once the player knows the game; see section 2) is the one concrete example found of letting experts change how disclosure behaves.

### Gaps
- NN/G's article was not opened; the two-level warning and the "obvious route" criterion rest on recall.
- Material Design: not searched (budget) and not fetchable. I recall no dedicated Material 3 article titled progressive disclosure; guidance is spread across components (expansion panels, bottom sheets, steppers). Unverified.
- Raph Koster ("A Theory of Fun", fun as pattern learning; boredom when the pattern is exhausted or noise when it cannot be parsed): no source retrieved in this session.
- The Carroll and Carrasco study itself was not retrieved; its numbers are not given here for that reason.
- No study was found that quantifies undiscovered-feature rates or expert slowdown from disclosure in games.

## 2. How complex strategy and sim games stage depth, and which patterns survive on a phone

### Takeaway
The best-evidenced desktop patterns are nested, lockable tooltips for terms and number breakdowns (CK3), context-triggered lessons that fire when a concept first becomes relevant (RimWorld), and designing the rules themselves so that everything can be shown clearly (Into the Breach). Hover-dependent patterns degrade without a mouse: CK3 on console had to hide nested tooltips behind a mode, and Balatro and Gladiabots both needed fixes for tooltips on phones.

### Cited Findings
- Paradox's Victoria 3 dev diary #29 on user experience (desktop, 2021 or 2022; date not shown in the snippet) describes nested tooltips as a technique "made famous by Crusader Kings 3" and says the UX team uses them for two jobs: explaining game concepts and breaking down how a number was reached. [snippet] — [Victoria 3 Dev Diary #29](https://forum.paradoxplaza.com/forum/developer-diary/victoria-3-dev-diary-29-user-experience.1506484/)
- CK3's tooltips must "lock" so the pointer can move into them. The CK3 dev diary (quoted on the Obsidian forum; desktop, about 2020) says the default is a timer lock, with an action lock available; the timer length is a setting, and middle-click locks manually. The stated aim is that related information is "a mouse move away" whenever blue highlighted text appears, and the alternative lock mode exists so players can switch to an opt-in style once they know the game. A forum reply on the Victoria 3 diary lists the candidate lock designs as mouse tendency, timer lock and action lock. [snippet] — [Obsidian forum quoting the CK3 dev diary](https://forum.obsidian.md/t/nested-popovers/573); [Victoria 3 Dev Diary #29, page 5](https://forum.paradoxplaza.com/forum/developer-diary/victoria-3-dev-diary-29-user-experience.1506484/page-5)
- Philip Ardeljan's 2020 analysis "Tooltips in tooltips" recommends limiting nesting to something like two or three levels and lists accessibility and touch screens as open problems for the pattern on the web. [snippet] — [philip.design](https://philip.design/blog/tooltips-in-tooltips)
- CK3 on consoles (March 2022, controller): Paradox's console dev diary says the team rejected keeping the PC interface with the thumbstick as a virtual mouse and rebuilt the UI for controllers. Reviews report that descriptions pop up automatically when highlighting some things, but the nested tooltip system is "hidden behind a specific tooltip mode", and that PC handles multiple layers more easily. Console builds show context-sensitive control hints in the bottom-left corner. A patch (1.006) had to fix how tooltips are closed (shoulder buttons, then the right face button). [snippet] — [Paradox console dev diary #3](https://www.paradoxinteractive.com/games/crusader-kings-iii/news/ck3-console-dev-diary-3-uiux-and-controls); [TheSixthAxis review](https://thesixthaxis.com/2022/03/29/crusader-kings-3-console-review); [Wccftech impressions](https://wccftech.com/crusader-kings-iii-console-ps5-xbox-series-x-impressions/); [console update notes](https://devtrackers.gg/crusader-kings/p/082e2861-console-edition-update-4-may-16-2022)
- Dwarf Fortress's Steam edition (desktop, 6 December 2022) is the largest case of retrofitting an interface onto a deep simulation. Before it, Tarn Adams said the mouse worked on "like 20% of the screens". The Steam version added native mouse control, tooltips, a new graphical UI and, after 16 years, a tutorial covering only the main early roadblocks: controls, mining, stockpiles and zones. [snippet] — [PC Gamer on the tutorial](https://www.pcgamer.com/the-pcs-most-infamously-complex-game-gets-a-tutorial-after-16-years); [PC Gamer on mouse support](https://www.pcgamer.com/tutorials-and-mouse-support-could-make-dwarf-fortress-on-steam-vastly-easier-to-play)
- Outcome figures for that release: publisher Kitfox had forecast 162,905 Steam units in the first two months; about 160,000 sold in the first 24 hours; peak concurrent players reached 22,324 (SteamDB). [snippet] — [Game World Observer](https://gameworldobserver.com/2022/12/08/dwarf-fortress-sales-160k-units-24-hours-steam)
- Reviewers' reservations about the same release: clicking to designate and inspect was welcome, but "the new UI struggles to accommodate every aspect of this bottomless game"; one reviewer called the tutorial both a crucial addition and the source of their strongest reservations; Adventure mode was left out at launch. [snippet] — [PC Gamer review](https://www.pcgamer.com/dwarf-fortress-review/)
- RimWorld's "learning helper" (desktop) sits in the top-right corner; a lesson is activated and shown when something happens that relates to a concept the player has not yet learned. Mods can add lessons. [snippet] — [RimWorld Wiki: Learning helper](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Learning_helper)
- Into the Breach (desktop 2018; later ports): co-designer Justin Ma, "As a game design principle, we would sacrifice cool ideas for the sake of clarity every time", and "If it can be shown in a clear way, [then] yes, we can have that." The team wanted every death to feel like the player's own fault, which made fully telegraphed enemy attacks the core mechanic. [snippet] — [Game Developer, 2018](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/-i-into-the-breach-i-dev-on-ui-design-sacrifice-cool-ideas-for-the-sake-of-clarity-every-time-)
- A later developer who studied the Into the Breach postmortems reports that Subset Games restricted the design itself to keep the UI clear, for example limiting how many status effects a single move can apply. The GDC 2019 design postmortem by Matthew Davis is the primary source (paywalled; not retrieved). [snippet] — [Game Developer deep dive on Beastieball's UI](https://gamedeveloper.com/art/deep-dive-developing-a-clear-and-consistent-visual-language-in-the-ui-of-beastieball); [GDC Vault session](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1026333/)
- Balatro on phones (released 26 September 2024; touch): Engadget called it "an almost perfect mobile port" and praised the reworked drag and drop. The same port needed a patch for tooltips that "were sometimes outside of screen" and an increase in the size of most text; some players still report the UI as too small on a standard iPhone and long Joker descriptions as cramped on small phones. A desktop mod that switches on the "mobile UI" shows the phone interface is a separate layer over the same game. [snippet] — [Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/gaming/balatro-is-an-almost-perfect-mobile-port-163050971.html); [App Store listing and patch notes](https://apps.apple.com/ie/app/balatro/id6502453075); [Reddit thread on UI size](https://lr.sudovanilla.org/r/iosgaming/comments/1fvz8fd/how_is_the_small_looking_ui_on_balatro_mobile_on)
- Marvel Snap (phone-first, portrait, 2022 onward) stages its card vocabulary through a "collection level" track: Pool 1 runs from collection level 18 to 214, by which point every Pool 1 card is owned; one guide puts the first "wall" at level 166, where cards go from one every four levels to one every six; later pools replace fixed unlocks with random caches; past level 1,500 a new card takes about 96 levels, and players at 2,500+ report still missing Pool 3 cards. [snippet; which guide carries which figure is not clear from the snippets] — [Marvel Snap Zone progression guide](https://marvelsnapzone.com/marvel-snap-progression-guide/); [Untapped account progression](https://blog.snap.untapped.gg/marvel-snap-wiki-account-progression); [Hearthstone Top Decks guide](https://www.hearthstonetopdecks.com/the-hearthstone-players-guide-to-marvel-snap/)
- The Marvel Snap team has revised the track more than once (a Ben Brode explanation of collection-level changes; a later "Snap Packs" system described as additive, with the earn-cards-as-you-play journey for new players kept). [snippet] — [Marvel Snap Zone: Brode on the collection level road](https://marvelsnapzone.com/update-to-the-collection-level-road-ben-brode-explains-the-changes/); [Marvel Snap: Snap Packs](https://marvelsnap.com/?p=90152)
- Factorio's developers wrote a Friday Facts post (#241) titled "New player experience"; only community replies surfaced, not its content. [snippet] — [Factorio forums, FFF #241 thread](https://forums.factorio.com/viewtopic.php?p=360665)

### Inferences
- What translates to a portrait phone, judged from the cases above:

| Pattern | Origin | On touch |
|---|---|---|
| Nested tooltips for terms and number breakdowns | Desktop hover (CK3) | Hover does not exist. CK3 console moved it behind a mode and reviewers found it weaker. The touch equivalent is tap on a highlighted word to open a sheet, with linked words inside it, a visible back path, and a depth of two or three at most (Ardeljan). |
| Tooltip lock (timer or explicit action) | Desktop (CK3) | The lock problem disappears on touch, since a tapped sheet stays open; the new problem is dismissal and going back, which CK3 console had to patch. |
| Context-triggered lesson when a concept first matters | Desktop (RimWorld) | Platform-neutral. Needs only a small, dismissible card. |
| Rules constrained so that everything can be shown | Desktop (Into the Breach) | Platform-neutral and more valuable on a small screen, where less can be shown. |
| Long card or part descriptions | Desktop (Balatro) | Works but strains: off-screen tooltips and small text were the two fixes Balatro's phone port needed. |
| Vocabulary staged through an account track | Phone (Marvel Snap) | Native to phones. The early, deterministic stretch is praised less than the late, slow, random stretch is complained about. |

- The Dwarf Fortress numbers show demand for an approachable face on a deep simulation, but they cannot be attributed to the interface alone (twenty years of reputation and new graphics arrived together), and reviewers agree the new UI still does not cover the whole game. The modest tutorial scope (four basics) is the transferable part.
- The Into the Breach restriction is a design rule, not a UI rule: cap how much one element can do so that its full effect fits in one glance. For a behaviour editor this argues for capping conditions per instinct and effects per word so that a rule can always be read in one line.

### Gaps
- Not sourced this session, so not cited: Baldur's Gate 3's nested tooltips, Civilization's advisors and tech-gated interface, Factorio's and Dyson Sphere Program's tutorial and research-gated menus, Oxygen Not Included's overlays, Slay the Spire's keyword tooltips and its small batches of unlocks per character. I have general knowledge of each but no retrieved source.
- No GDC talk by Paradox on CK3's tooltips was found; the evidence is dev diaries and third-party analysis.
- No designer statement was found for Balatro's or Slay the Spire's tooltip design.
- The CK3 console "tooltip mode" was not described in detail in any snippet (which button, how nesting is navigated).

## 3. Programming and logic games and tools for non-programmers: one concept at a time, limited palettes, seeing the program run, and touch research

### Takeaway
The consistent practice is a deliberately small vocabulary (ScratchJr cut Scratch's 100+ blocks to about 30), shapes or sentence slots that make invalid programs impossible, short guided steps with unaided checks in between (Game Builder Garage: about five minutes per step, checkpoints between lessons), and a large controlled study shows tutorials pay off only when the system is too complex to discover by experiment. Touch-specific research is thin: it shows drag and drop is the weak point on small screens and that tap-to-select then tap-to-place is a workable alternative, but I found no study giving palette-size or target-size numbers for phones.

### Cited Findings
- Andersen et al., "The Impact of Tutorials on Games of Varying Complexity" (CHI 2012; web games on desktop): a multivariate experiment across three games (Refraction, Hello Worlds, Foldit), eight tutorial designs, over 45,000 players. Tutorials increased play time by as much as 29% in the most complex game (Foldit) and did not significantly improve engagement in the two simpler games. The authors conclude that investment in tutorials may not be justified for games whose mechanics can be discovered through experimentation. [snippet] — [Project page](https://grail.cs.washington.edu/projects/game-abtesting/chi2012/); [PDF](https://grail.cs.washington.edu/projects/game-abtesting/chi2012/chi2012.pdf)
- From the same paper as I recall it: the variables were tutorial presence, context-sensitivity, freedom and availability of on-demand help; in Foldit tutorials also raised progress (levels completed) by about 75%; restricting player freedom showed no benefit; on-demand help gave mixed results across the three games. [recall] — [PDF](https://grail.cs.washington.edu/projects/game-abtesting/chi2012/chi2012.pdf)
- ScratchJr (Flannery et al., IDC 2013; tablets, young children, given as ages 4 to 6 in one snippet and 5 to 7 in the project's own material as I recall): the design table lists Scratch's 100+ blocks as "overwhelming and not necessary for an early childhood programming language", with ScratchJr at roughly 30. (A secondary citation says "one third fewer blocks"; the design table implies fewer than a third as many. The table is the primary source.) Blocks are primarily icon-based, with words shown on demand. Blocks look like jigsaw pieces whose visual properties correspond to their syntactic properties, so a syntax error cannot be made. [snippet] — [Designing ScratchJr, IDC 2013 (PDF)](https://sites.bc.edu/devtech/wp-content/uploads/sites/181/2018/02/scratchjr_idc_2013.pdf); [MIT Media Lab record](https://www.media.mit.edu/publications/designing-scratchjr-support-for-early-childhood-learning-through-computer-programming/)
- During ScratchJr's design, teachers asked for an "off switch" to hide portions of the block palette, which made the team think through the teaching philosophy behind the palette layout. [snippet] — [Designing ScratchJr, IDC 2013 (PDF)](https://sites.bc.edu/devtech/wp-content/uploads/sites/181/2018/02/scratchjr_idc_2013.pdf)
- A 2023 follow-up (Blake-West and Bers, International Journal of Child-Computer Interaction) evaluates ScratchJr's "low floor, high ceiling" goal against usage data. Its findings did not appear in the snippets. [snippet] — [ScratchJr design in practice (PDF)](https://sites.bc.edu/devtech/wp-content/uploads/sites/181/2023/07/ScratchJr-design-in-practice-Low-fl_2023_International-Journal-of-Child-Com.pdf)
- Google's Blockly design guidance (desktop-first, with touch support): prefer high-level blocks; do not convert an entire API into blocks; pick the input method (dropdown, text field or value block) by user need; put conditionals and loops in separate, differently coloured categories because they look alike but behave differently; avoid "mutator" pop-ups for younger or novice users because they are not intuitive; and, because blocks make programming visual and tangible, take care not to suggest interactions that are not supported. Blockly is described as a way over the initial learning curve, not a replacement for text languages. [snippet] — [Blockly: Block design](https://developers.google.com/blockly/guides/design/blocks?hl=en); [Blockly: Best practices](https://developers.google.cn/blockly/guides/app-integration/best-practices?hl=en)
- Neil Fraser's "Ten things we've learned from Blockly" (2015) lists the team's lessons; its own abstract describes them as noncontroversial folk knowledge without supporting data. [snippet, from a library catalogue record of the paper] — [catalogue record](https://ersearch2.cvtisr.sk/vufind/SummonRecord/FETCH-LOGICAL-i208t-1b48ac3a6a2ca9bcfa01a857972c91023ef1e772992d0cb6eb2af5d179fe8b593); [Blockly team publications](https://docs.blockly.com/publications/publications/)
- Touch and small screens:
  - A 2024 ACM paper on a mobile block-based environment reports students perceive block coding as slower than typing because of dragging and dropping. This is perception, not a timed measurement. [snippet] — [University of Luxembourg repository, DOI 10.1145/3649217.3653549](https://orbilu.uni.lu/bitstream/10993/61605/1/3649217.3653549.pdf)
  - Milne and Ladner's Blocks4All (CHI 2018, iPad) notes that custom visual elements such as blocks and gesture interactions such as drag and drop are often not accessible, and describes alternatives: double-tap-and-hold to reach the underlying drag, and a "select, select, drop" sequence (choose the block, choose the destination, place). [snippet] — [Blocks4All (PDF)](https://milnel2.github.io/files/Blocks4All.pdf)
  - A 2015 usability study of Blockly on tablets and phones reports that draggable blocks "showed potential" on those devices and calls for further usability measurement on mobile. [snippet] — [ICOFE 2015 abstract](https://www.hkmu.edu.hk/static_content/icofe2015/abs/061.html)
  - Scratch 3.0 (2019) added touch support for dragging and tapping blocks; Hopscotch is a phone and iPad tool built around dragging blocks into scripts. [snippet] — [Scratch 3.0 on mobile devices (course page)](https://www.lynda.com/Scratch-tutorials/Scratch-3-0-mobile-devices/2804663/2233068-4.html); [Hopscotch (Wikipedia)](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hopscotch_(programming_language))
- Human Resource Machine (Tomorrow Corporation, 2015; desktop, tablet and phone): Kyle Gabler said beginners and non-programmers "can get really scared of the idea of programming", so the game and everything around it was made simple and accessible. Press credits its "carefully constrained challenges and clear visualizations", and it is credited with distilling assembly programming into an easily understood visual metaphor. [snippet] — [Game Developer interview, 2015](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/why-a-i-world-of-goo-i-dev-made-a-puzzle-game-about-programming-humans); [Boing Boing, 2015](https://boingboing.net/2015/10/19/human-resource-machine-game.html?noamp=mobile); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_Resource_Machine)
- How Human Resource Machine does it, as I recall from the game: the whole language is about eleven commands; each level's palette lists only the commands allowed in that level, so a new command appears as one new tile; the program is a vertical list assembled by dragging; jumps are drawn as arrows; step, back and speed controls sit under the running program and the current line is highlighted while the worker acts it out. [recall; the surfaced sources do not describe this level of detail] — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_Resource_Machine)
- Nintendo's Game Builder Garage (Switch, 2021; controller, touch and mouse), from Nintendo's "Ask the Developer" interview:
  - Seven interactive lessons, each divided into steps designed to take about five minutes. [snippet]
  - Between lessons are "checkpoints": puzzle-like problems the player solves alone, to confirm understanding. [snippet]
  - The instructor "Bob" gives explicit directions ("Put a Nodon here", "Connect this Nodon to that Nodon"); a separate reference, "Alice's Guide", explains how each Nodon works for those who want more. [snippet]
  - The lessons are designed "so that even first graders can make games while having fun"; the developers cite complaints about other programming tools that people could not get to the end of the tutorials. [snippet]
  - Games built in lessons are saved into Free Programming so players can copy and modify them. Free Programming is capped at 512 Nodon and 1,024 connections. [snippet]
  — [Ask the Developer Vol. 1, Part 1](https://www.nintendo.com/us/whatsnew/ask-the-developer-vol-1-game-builder-garage-part-1/); [Part 2](https://www.nintendo.com/us/whatsnew/ask-the-developer-vol-1-game-builder-garage-part-2/); [Part 3](https://www.nintendo.com/us/whatsnew/ask-the-developer-vol-1-game-builder-garage-part-3/)
- Trigger-action ("if this then that") programming, the nearest research analogue to a "notice -> do" sentence: Ur et al. (CHI 2014, web study) report that inexperienced users can quickly learn to create programs containing multiple triggers or actions, and conclude that trigger-action programming with multiple triggers and actions is practical for the smart home. A press account of the study says 78% of the behaviours people asked for could be expressed as a single trigger and a single action. A Brown University summary says the results suggest it is simple enough for non-programmers. [snippet] — [ACM DOI 10.1145/2556288.2557420](https://www.doi.org/10.1145/2556288.2557420); [Brown CS blog, 2014](https://blog.cs.brown.edu/2014/04/29/michael-littman-chi2014-programming-smart-home/)

### Inferences
- Andersen's result is a direct argument for making the first editor experience discoverable by poking at it, and for spending tutorial effort only on the parts that cannot be discovered that way (in this kind of game, likely moods and rule priority, not placing a first instinct).
- The Ur finding (78% of wanted behaviours are one trigger, one action) supports making the bare two-slot sentence the whole first layer, with conditions as the second.
- Three independent sources (ScratchJr's jigsaw shapes, Blockly's guidance, trigger-action sentence slots) converge on error prevention by construction: the editor should offer only words that fit the slot being filled, so that an invalid sentence cannot be built and no error message is ever needed.
- ScratchJr's cut from 100+ to about 30 was for five-to-seven-year-olds and should not be read as a ceiling for adults; the transferable point is that the visible palette at any moment was kept small, and that teachers wanted a way to hide parts of it. A 60 to 100 word vocabulary implies the palette must be filtered by slot and by unlock state, never shown whole.
- On phones, dragging is the documented weak point (perceived slowness, accessibility), and "select, then choose destination" is the documented alternative. For a sentence with fixed slots this becomes: tap the slot, pick from a short list.
- Game Builder Garage's structure (explicit hand-holding, then an unaided checkpoint, then the result saved as an editable project) is the most complete worked example found of taking non-programmers through a node editor. That it needed seven lessons of step-by-step direction is itself weak evidence that free-form node graphs are harder to enter than sentence slots.

### Gaps
- No study was found that compares sentence-like blocks with node graphs for novices, on any device. The comparison above is indirect.
- No study was found giving numbers for palette size or drag-target size for block editors on phones. General platform touch-target guidance exists (Apple, Material) but was not retrieved.
- Not sourced this session: 7 Billion Humans, while True: learn(), Opus Magnum and other Zachtronics games (their histograms comparing a solution with other players' on cost, speed and size), RoboZZle, Lightbot, Baba Is You, Scratch's own palette design, LEGO SPIKE and Boost apps, Swift Playgrounds, Apple Shortcuts, Zapier.
- Recalled but not retrieved, so not cited as findings: Weintrop and Wilensky (IDC 2015, and a 2017 classroom comparison) found students see blocks as easier than text because labels read like natural language, shapes and colours signal use, the palette supports recognition over recall, and dragging prevents syntax errors, while also seeing blocks as slower and less powerful for larger programs. This matches the 2024 student-perception snippet above.
- The Andersen detail (progress figure, on-demand help results) rests on recall.

## 4. Sentence-builder and rule-card interfaces: Gambits, Tactics, Gladiabots, The Bibites, smart-home rules; learnability, frustration, priorities and conflicts

### Takeaway
Ordered "condition -> action" lists are repeatedly described as learnable and as a simplified programming language, and the main recorded frustration is not the format but starving the player of rule words for too long (FFXII) or bolting the system onto a game not built around it (Dragon Age: Origins). Evidence on how these games make priority and conflicts visible is thin in what could be retrieved: vertical order as priority is the standard, and Gladiabots' community needs a wiki page to explain evaluation order.

### Cited Findings
- Final Fantasy XII's Gambits (console, 2006; The Zodiac Age remaster 2017): the idea descends from Hiroyuki Ito's monster AI in Final Fantasy IV, which Square Enix's Takashi Katano called somewhat similar to the Gambit system. The team adopted gambits because adding real-time action to command battles risked being too fast-paced and difficult; Katano said the team "didn't want it to be overwhelming". [snippet] — [Game Developer: why FFIV was key to FFXII's gambit system](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/why-i-final-fantasy-iv-i-was-key-to-i-ffxii-i-s-ai-driven-gambit-system); [RPG Site interview, 2017](https://www.rpgsite.net/interview/5595-final-fantasy-xii-the-zodiac-age-interview-developers-on-tactical-gameplay-cheats-legacy-and-more)
- Hiroaki Kato described the fun of gambits as thinking about how efficiently you could build them to fight to advantage. Retronauts described the system as "almost like a simplified programming language". [snippet] — [RPG Site interview, 2017](https://www.rpgsite.net/interview/5595-final-fantasy-xii-the-zodiac-age-interview-developers-on-tactical-gameplay-cheats-legacy-and-more); [Retronauts](https://retronauts.com/article/421/the-trials-tribulations-and-trial-mode-of-final-fantasy-xii-the-zodiac-age?amphtml=1)
- Player-side frustration with pacing: one writer did not like FFXII "at all until around the 15 hour mark" when more than the most basic gambits became available. A 2022 comparison blog nonetheless judges FFXII's system "a little more accessible to a first time player" than Dragon Age: Origins', because gambits are introduced slowly, while Origins' tactics are "fairly bare boned when introduced". [snippet; blog sources, single voices] — [GNG Games blog, 2015](https://gnggames.blogspot.com/2015/02/final-fantasy-xii-dragon-age-origins.html); [RPG Tourist blog, 2022](https://rpgtourist.blogspot.com/2022/09/exploring-tactical-systems-in-final.html)
- Continued appetite for the format: PC Gamer ran a piece arguing modern developers should "take another pass at FF12's gambit system". [snippet, headline only; date not shown] — [PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/games/final-fantasy/turn-based-rpgs-are-officially-back-in-fashion-but-i-want-more-its-time-for-modern-developers-to-take-another-pass-at-ff12s-gambit-system)
- Dragon Age: Origins' Tactics (desktop and console, 2009): reviewers describe a "pre-programmed set of hierarchical tactics" and generally judge it a weaker take on gambits: "somewhat limited — and the game certainly wasn't built around them in the same way FF12 was"; one says the idea "had worked near flawlessly" in FFXII but the Origins implementation "was nowhere near as good". [snippet] — [RPG Tourist blog, 2022](https://rpgtourist.blogspot.com/2022/09/exploring-tactical-systems-in-final.html); [RPGFan review](https://www.rpgfan.com/review/dragon-age-origins-4/); [DarkZero review](https://darkzero.co.uk/?p=17458)
- Gladiabots (GFX47; desktop and phones/tablets; IGF 2020 entrant): the player builds each robot's AI in a visual editor and then watches the match without control. The developer's description is "simple to use but satisfyingly deep"; a community write-up describes the goal as defining a decision tree that behaves better than the enemy's. [snippet] — [IGF entry](https://igf.com/gladiabots); [App Store listing](https://apps.apple.com/app/gladiabots/id1469623947); [community write-up](https://reddit.birdcat.cafe/r/pireThoughts/wiki/pier4r_oc/2016/12/gladiabots_teach_concepts)
- Gladiabots details relevant to touch and to visible priority: an update "unlocked node selection in 'Attack' mission to enable node tooltips on mobile", which shows node tooltips depend on selecting a node on touch and had been unavailable in an early mission; the editor supports comment nodes; the official wiki has a "debug console" topic; the community wiki has an entry on "priority evaluation of the AI"; one community note calls the in-game editor "relatively limited". [snippet] — [App Store update notes](https://apps.apple.com/il/app/gladiabots/id1469623947); [changelog 1.4.16](https://gfx47.itch.io/gladiabots/devlog/139657/1416-changelog-latest-dev-version); [official wiki](https://wiki.gladiabots.com); [subreddit wiki index](https://reddit.birdcat.cafe/r/Gladiabots/wiki/index)
- Smart-home rule builders (IFTTT-style): non-programmers quickly learned to write rules with multiple triggers or actions, and most wanted behaviours needed only one trigger and one action (Ur et al., CHI 2014; see section 3). [snippet] — [ACM DOI 10.1145/2556288.2557420](https://www.doi.org/10.1145/2556288.2557420); [Brown CS blog, 2014](https://blog.cs.brown.edu/2014/04/29/michael-littman-chi2014-programming-smart-home/)

### Inferences
- The FFXII record separates two things: the rule format was understood (players and press describe it readily as a simple programming language), while the unlock schedule for rule words was the complaint. A small starting vocabulary is fine; a starting vocabulary too small to build anything interesting for many hours is the failure.
- The Origins criticism ("not built around them") suggests that a rule editor is judged by whether the game's outcomes visibly depend on it. A rule system offered as optional automation gets compared unfavourably with one that is the game.
- That Gladiabots' community maintains a page on priority evaluation is weak evidence that evaluation order is not self-evident from a tree layout. Ordered vertical lists (Gambits, Tactics) carry priority in position, which costs nothing extra on a portrait screen.
- Strength-weighted instincts (as opposed to strict top-to-bottom order) have no close precedent in the sources retrieved; the games found all resolve conflicts by order. That makes showing "which instinct won and by how much" a new interface problem, not a borrowed one.

### Gaps
- The Bibites' brain editor: two searches returned nothing about the game. Not covered.
- Bot Land, Screeps-style games, Autonauts, Carnage Heart: not searched (budget).
- No Steam review text or learning-curve data for Gladiabots was retrieved; how its debug view and replays work is unconfirmed. From recall only: priority runs left to right among sibling nodes, and a bot's active path can be inspected during a replay. Unverified.
- No primary description was retrieved of how Gambits and Tactics order rules, how slots are unlocked, or of the remaster's change to gambit availability. From recall only: both evaluate top to bottom and fire the first rule whose condition holds; each rule has an on/off toggle; FFXII's rule slots come from the licence board and, in the 2006 original, conditions were bought or found piecemeal, which The Zodiac Age relaxed by selling nearly all of them early; Origins ties slot count to level and a skill, and ships preset rule lists per role. Unverified.
- Brackenbury et al., "How Users Interpret Bugs in Trigger-Action Programming" (CHI 2019), surfaced only as a reference listing inside the results for the Ur et al. search; the listing says that as if-then rule systems are used in more complex scenarios, users must be able to identify bugs in them. The page hosting the listing could not be identified from the snippet, so it is not cited as a finding.
- Recalled but not retrieved, and directly relevant to "notice -> do" semantics:
  - Huang and Cakmak (UbiComp 2015) found people confuse triggers that are events ("when it starts raining") with triggers that are states ("while it is raining"), and actions that are instant with actions that persist, and that this ambiguity causes errors.
  - Brackenbury et al. (CHI 2019) catalogued rule-bug classes including priority conflicts between rules, contradictory actions, repeated triggering, and "missing reversal" (people assume "if dark, light on" also turns the light off when it stops being dark), and found people poor at predicting these.
  Both would bear on whether an instinct fires once or continuously, and on what happens when a mood stops. Neither was verified in this session.

## 5. Unlock pacing: how much new per session, "one new thing at a time", avoiding slow starts and overload

### Takeaway
No source gave a tested number of new elements per session. What is documented is a set of consistent practices (one new element per short level, steps of about five minutes, peripheral systems held back until the core loop is learned, each idea introduced safely before being developed and twisted) and one clear failure mode: unlocks tied to the calendar or stretched over many hours are resented and get patched, while unlocks tied to what the player has done are accepted.

### Cited Findings
- George Fan, "How I Got My Mom to Play Through Plants vs. Zombies" (GDC 2012; PvZ shipped on desktop in 2009 and later on phones): Fan credited the tutorial as the main reason the game reached casual players ("If the tutorial hadn't been done well… then the other elements of the game wouldn't have mattered"). The team introduced peripheral mechanics very slowly; money does not come into play until the player has finished 10 levels. On-screen text was held to about eight words at a time ("Click on a seed packet to pick it up", not "Hi, welcome to Plants vs. Zombies!"). [snippet] — [Game Developer write-up](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/gdc-2012-10-tutorial-tips-from-i-plants-vs-zombies-i-creator-george-fan); [GDC Vault session](https://gdcvault.com/play/1015541/); [notes on the talk](https://notes.hamatti.org/sources/talks/how-i-got-my-mom-to-play-through-plants-vs.-zombies)
- Fan's ten tips, as I recall the list from that write-up: blend the tutorial into the game; have the player do rather than read; spread out the teaching of mechanics; get the player to do a thing just once; use fewer words; use unobtrusive messaging; use adaptive messaging (only prompt players who show they need it); do not create noise; use visuals to teach; leverage what people already know. In the game itself, new plants arrive roughly one per level. [recall; the snippets confirm tips one and five and the slow introduction] — [Game Developer write-up](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/gdc-2012-10-tutorial-tips-from-i-plants-vs-zombies-i-creator-george-fan)
- Fan on why the start must be light and later stages can carry more: willingness to learn is small at first and grows as the player becomes invested. [snippet] — [Game Developer write-up](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/gdc-2012-10-tutorial-tips-from-i-plants-vs-zombies-i-creator-george-fan)
- Game Builder Garage (2021): each lesson step is designed to take about five minutes, lessons build step by step, and an unaided checkpoint sits between lessons. [snippet] — [Ask the Developer Vol. 1, Part 3](https://www.nintendo.com/us/whatsnew/ask-the-developer-vol-1-game-builder-garage-part-3/)
- Nintendo's four-step level structure (kishotenketsu): Koichi Hayashida, director of Super Mario 3D Land and co-director of 3D World, says the structure guiding level design is based on the four-act narrative form kishotenketsu: introduction, development, twist, resolution. He said Super Mario Galaxy 2 was the first game where the philosophy became clear to him (2012 interview). Coverage of 3D World describes mechanics introduced so that the game is "constantly teaching you how to approach new sorts of problems while subtly (and simultaneously) changing those same problems". [snippet; the original 2012 Gamasutra interview was not retrieved] — [Nintendo Life, 2012](https://www.nintendolife.com/news/2012/04/super_mario_3d_land_director_shares_level_design_inspirations); [Kotaku, 2015](https://kotaku.com/what-made-super-mario-3d-world-so-great-1691916400); [Engadget, 2015](https://www.engadget.com/2015/03/17/super-mario-3d-world-design/); [MCV on the Game Maker's Toolkit video](https://www.mcvuk.com/development/video-nintendos-level-design-secrets-in-four-steps)
- Super Mario Maker (Wii U with touch screen, September 2015), the clearest case of unlock pacing going wrong for a creation tool:
  - As designed, players needed nine days of play to receive every tool; each day's delivery required five minutes of creating in the editor. [snippet]
  - The community responded negatively, calling the mechanic unnecessary; an editorial of the time was headed "slow unlocking feels more frustrating than fun". [snippet]
  - A launch patch (1.01) let tools unlock by placing enough blocks, reported as roughly 15 minutes per batch and the full set within a couple of hours; the nine-day route still works. Reports conflict on how much faster it really is (one outlet argued you still must fill screens with each new unlock). [snippet]
  - Producer Takashi Tezuka's stated reason for gating at all (the snippet spells the name "Takeshi"): unlocking too many tools at the start would leave some players feeling overwhelmed. This comes from a secondary report, not a verified Nintendo statement. [snippet]
  — [GamesRadar](https://gamesradar.com/super-mario-maker-update-unlocks); [Nintendo Life](https://www.nintendolife.com/news/2015/09/super_mario_maker_patch_speeds_up_tool_delivery); [GamesBeat](https://gamesbeat.com/super-mario-maker-patch-removes-item-unlock-delay-now-15-minutes-not-1-day-per-batch/); [Cubed3](https://cubed3.com/news/24907/1/super-mario-maker-removes-nine-day-system.html); [Nintendo World Report editorial](https://nintendoworldreport.com/editorial/40974/super-mario-makers-slow-unlocking-feels-more-frustrating-than-fun); [My Nintendo News on Tezuka's reason](https://mynintendonews.com/2015/09/27/nintendo-explains-why-not-all-mario-maker-tools-were-available-at-start/)
- Marvel Snap's cadence (phone): in the first pool a new card arrives about every four collection levels, slowing to every six at the first wall (level 166), with all first-pool cards owned by level 214; much later, a new card can take about 96 levels, which is where complaints concentrate. [snippet] — [Marvel Snap Zone progression guide](https://marvelsnapzone.com/marvel-snap-progression-guide/); [Untapped account progression](https://blog.snap.untapped.gg/marvel-snap-wiki-account-progression)
- Final Fantasy XII: a player reports not enjoying the game until about hour 15, when enough gambits were available (see section 4). [snippet] — [GNG Games blog, 2015](https://gnggames.blogspot.com/2015/02/final-fantasy-xii-dragon-age-origins.html)
- Dwarf Fortress's tutorial (2022) limits itself to four early roadblocks: controls, mining, stockpiles, zones. [snippet] — [PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/the-pcs-most-infamously-complex-game-gets-a-tutorial-after-16-years)
- Andersen et al. (CHI 2012): tutorials improved engagement only in the most complex of three games, which bears on where to spend guided teaching versus leaving players to experiment. [snippet] — [Project page](https://grail.cs.washington.edu/projects/game-abtesting/chi2012/)

### Inferences
- The Mario Maker episode gives a usable rule: gate by activity, never by the calendar, and make the fast path available to those who want it. Nintendo kept gradual unlocking (Tezuka's overwhelm concern stood) but changed the trigger from days to use, and a unit of roughly 15 minutes of real use per batch was acceptable where a day was not.
- Across PvZ (about one new plant per level, a level being a few minutes), Game Builder Garage (one concept per five-minute step) and Mario Maker after the patch (a batch per 15 minutes of use), the common rhythm is one new element or small batch per 5 to 15 minutes of active play, with each new element used at once. This is a pattern across practice, not a measured optimum.
- PvZ holding the shop back for 10 levels suggests the same treatment for a shared body-and-brain budget: let players build with a generous or invisible budget first, and introduce the trade-off once the building itself is familiar.
- Kishotenketsu applied to vocabulary: a new word is first met where it cannot go wrong, then used in a harder case, then combined with an older word in an unexpected way, then left in the palette. The "twist" step is where combination depth comes from, and it needs no new vocabulary.
- For a small group of friends who play at different rates, calendar-independent unlocks also avoid punishing the keenest player, which is exactly the group Mario Maker's original design annoyed.

### Gaps
- No study was found that measures how many new elements per session players absorb. Working-memory limits are often cited for this in UX writing, but I retrieved no source applying a tested number to game unlocks.
- Supercell and King practices: not searched (budget). No sourced claims.
- Fan's full ten-tip list and the one-plant-per-level cadence rest on recall.
- The original 2012 Hayashida interview was not retrieved; the kishotenketsu findings rest on coverage of it.
- Mario Maker 2's choice to open most tools from the start is from recall only and was not checked.

## 6. Debuggability as part of the interface: "watch it think", replays, which rule fired, time scrubbing, comparison

### Takeaway
This is the thinnest section: few sources retrieved in this session describe debugging views directly. What can be cited is that the admired designs make the system's next action and the reasons for a number visible before and while it happens (Into the Breach's telegraphs, CK3's number breakdowns, Human Resource Machine's visualised execution), that Game Builder Garage separates "do as shown" from "prove you understood", and that rule-system research names bug-finding as the problem that grows with complexity.

### Cited Findings
- Into the Breach makes prediction part of the board: enemy attacks are fully telegraphed so that a loss is always traceable to the player's decision, and the designers cut or constrained mechanics that could not be shown clearly (for example capping status effects per move). [snippet] — [Game Developer, 2018](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/-i-into-the-breach-i-dev-on-ui-design-sacrifice-cool-ideas-for-the-sake-of-clarity-every-time-); [Game Developer deep dive on Beastieball's UI](https://gamedeveloper.com/art/deep-dive-developing-a-clear-and-consistent-visual-language-in-the-ui-of-beastieball)
- Paradox uses nested tooltips specifically for "detailed breakdowns of numbers", that is, answering "why is this value what it is" at the point where the value is shown (desktop). [snippet] — [Victoria 3 Dev Diary #29](https://forum.paradoxplaza.com/forum/developer-diary/victoria-3-dev-diary-29-user-experience.1506484/)
- Human Resource Machine is credited for "clear visualizations" of the running program, with office workers physically carrying out each instruction (desktop, tablet, phone; 2015). [snippet] — [Boing Boing, 2015](https://boingboing.net/2015/10/19/human-resource-machine-game.html?noamp=mobile)
- Game Builder Garage pairs guided construction with "checkpoints", small puzzles solved without guidance that are essentially debugging exercises on a prepared program, and offers a per-element reference (Alice's Guide) on demand. [snippet] — [Ask the Developer Vol. 1, Part 3](https://www.nintendo.com/us/whatsnew/ask-the-developer-vol-1-game-builder-garage-part-3/)
- Gladiabots on phones needed an update so that nodes could be selected in an early mission "to enable node tooltips on mobile"; inspection of a rule on touch depends on being able to select it. Its official wiki lists a debug console topic. [snippet] — [App Store update notes](https://apps.apple.com/il/app/gladiabots/id1469623947); [official wiki](https://wiki.gladiabots.com)
- Balatro's phone port had tooltips rendering off-screen until patched, a reminder that inspection pop-ups anchored to the inspected object fail at screen edges on small displays. [snippet] — [App Store listing and patch notes](https://apps.apple.com/ie/app/balatro/id6502453075)
- RimWorld's learning helper surfaces an explanation at the moment the related event happens, not in advance. [snippet] — [RimWorld Wiki: Learning helper](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Learning_helper)

### Inferences
- The common thread in the cited cases is that explanation is attached to the thing being explained, at the moment it matters: the telegraph is on the tile, the breakdown is on the number, the lesson appears when the event occurs. For a behaviour editor the equivalent is marking, on the creature and on the sentence, which instinct is acting now.
- On a portrait phone, a split view (world above, the creature's rule list below with the firing rule lit) uses the vertical order of rules as the debug display, with no extra screen. This follows from the vertical-list precedent in section 4 and the selection requirement Gladiabots ran into; it is a design inference, not something a source demonstrated.
- Inspection pop-ups should be sheets anchored to the screen edge, not to the object, given Balatro's off-screen tooltip fix.
- Into the Breach's approach implies a budget on what a single rule may do: if a rule's effect cannot be shown in one highlight, the rule is too complicated for the interface.

### Gaps
- Research on if-then rule systems names bug identification by end users as the problem that grows with rule-set complexity (Brackenbury et al., CHI 2019), but only a reference listing surfaced, with no identifiable page and no findings (see section 4 gaps).
- No source was retrieved on time scrubbing, replays or side-by-side comparison views in any of the named games. No sourced example of "which rule fired" highlighting was found, although I believe Gladiabots, Human Resource Machine and 7 Billion Humans each do a version of it.
- Recalled but not retrieved: Bret Victor's 2012 essay "Learnable Programming" (worrydream.com/LearnableProgramming) is the standard designer statement here; its principles are to make flow visible, make time tangible (scrubbing a timeline of the run), show the data, and let people create by reacting to what they see. Zachtronics games show histograms comparing a player's solution with everyone else's on several measures, and Opus Magnum exports a looping animation of the solution. Game Builder Garage lets the player flip between the game screen and the programming screen with one button. None verified this session.
- No evidence was found on how much debugging support changes retention or success for non-programmers in games.

## 7. Presets, templates and remixing: does starting from a working example lower the barrier

### Takeaway
The strongest evidence is from Scratch: in data on more than a million users, people who remix more go on to use a wider range of commands, and people first exposed to a concept through remixing are more likely to use it later, although the authors themselves toned down the strength of the claim. Nintendo built the same idea into Game Builder Garage by saving every lesson's result as an editable project.

### Cited Findings
- Dasgupta, Hale, Monroy-Hernández and Hill, "Remixing as a Pathway to Computational Thinking" (CSCW 2016; Scratch, mostly desktop): tested on more than 2.4 million projects shared by more than 1 million Scratch users. [snippet] — [MIT Media Lab record](https://www.media.mit.edu/publications/remixing-as-a-pathway-to-computational-thinking)
- Finding one: users who remix more have larger repertoires of programming commands, even after controlling for the number of projects and the amount of code they have shared. [snippet] — [MIT Media Lab record](https://www.media.mit.edu/publications/remixing-as-a-pathway-to-computational-thinking)
- Finding two: exposure to a computational-thinking concept through remixing is associated with a higher likelihood of using that concept later; the authors' group blog reports this held for each of the six concepts studied, with varying strength. [snippet] — [Community Data Science Collective blog](https://blog.communitydata.science/studying-the-relationship-between-remixing-learning/)
- Caveat: revision notes in the paper's repository say the authors removed the word "strong" and other qualifiers and acknowledged having overstated the findings. The study is observational, so it shows association, not that remixing causes learning. [snippet] — [paper repository commit](https://gitea.communitydata.science/collective/cdsc_examples_repository/commit/a3aa7637e0bcf5419ef744ad514e07eeb198b1e5)
- Game Builder Garage: the games made in the lessons are saved in the Free Programming area as the player's own, "so you can use them to make new games as well". [snippet] — [Ask the Developer Vol. 1, Part 3](https://www.nintendo.com/us/whatsnew/ask-the-developer-vol-1-game-builder-garage-part-3/)
- For rule builders, a second paper by Ur and colleagues (CHI 2016) analysed IFTTT's shared recipes at scale; as I recall, it covers roughly 200,000 published recipes and shows heavy reuse of recipes written by others. The DOI below appeared in the search results without a title; I believe it is this paper, but neither the title nor the figures were confirmed. [recall] — [ACM DOI 10.1145/2858036.2858556](https://www.doi.org/10.1145/2858036.2858556)

### Inferences
- The Scratch finding supports two separate features: starting from a working creature and changing it, and being able to open a friend's creature to see how it was built. The second is the one tied to picking up new concepts.
- In a game where words unlock gradually, a remixed design may contain words the player has not unlocked. The sources do not address this; the options (show it greyed, block the copy, or treat exposure as a preview) are a design decision, and the Scratch result suggests exposure before use is beneficial.
- Game Builder Garage's approach, where the tutorial's output is the player's first template, costs nothing extra and guarantees every player owns at least one working example.

### Gaps
- Mario Maker's course sharing, Dreams' remixable creations and the Apple Shortcuts gallery: no sources retrieved.
- No evidence was found on presets specifically for rule lists (for example Dragon Age: Origins' preset tactics by role) and whether players keep, edit or discard them.
- No evidence was found comparing "start blank" with "start from a template" for non-programmers in a game, as opposed to an association in a learning community.
- The IFTTT recipe-adoption figures rest on recall.
