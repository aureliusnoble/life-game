## 2. How the design process runs

Each area below goes through the same four steps, and is not called done until it passes.

1. **Write it down** as a design document.
2. **Test it for direction.** Where the stand-in world can run it, a check that each piece does something and that nothing is broken. Where it is about people, a mockup to react to. Tuning waits for the balance pass at the end.
3. **Questions and answers** on the choices that are yours.
4. **Close it** against its own checklist, and list what is still open.

## 3. How mechanics and balance are tested

There are two passes, and they ask different things. The mechanics pass asks whether everything does something and whether what we promised can be built. The balance pass asks whether the numbers are fair. The first comes before the screens are designed, because it can remove, merge or add parts and words. The second comes last.

### The mechanics pass

Asked for on 16 November. It covers bodies, brains, life cycles and societies (areas 2, 3, 5 and 6), and it is run on the frozen lists: the species sheet for bodies (area 13) and the vocabulary for brains.

**First question: does every part matter?** "Part" means every option on the species sheet that costs budget, and every piece a brain is written from: each sense, act, mood, mood function, signal and setting in `design/neural-blocks.md`. Each has to pass four tests. None of them is about balance.

| Test | Passes when |
|---|---|
| It changes something | The same design with and without it differs in what happens: what it eats, what eats it, where it can live, how many it leaves |
| It is worth choosing somewhere | In at least one setting, a design with it does better than the same design without it, by more than the noise from map to map. A setting is a habitat, a season, a set of neighbours or a way of living |
| It is not another part under a second name | There is a setting where it and its nearest neighbour give different results |
| Its effect can be seen | A player watching the tank could tell that it did something |

A part that fails is reworked, merged with its neighbour, given the mechanic it lacks, or cut. The first lab runs already give a starting list of failures: the sting, camouflage, false eyes and egg size changed nothing in any habitat, and three of the four insides, eyes on stalks and paddles were never in a best design.

**Second question: can what we envisaged be programmed?** The design documents promise particular ways of living. Each one has to be written out in full in the vocabulary, on a body the species sheet allows, within the allowance a player has, and then run.

| Where they are listed | What |
|---|---|
| `design/neural-blocks.md`, sections 11 and 12 | The tested recipes and the behaviour atlas |
| `design/social.md`, section 5 | About thirty-five societies and what each is built from |
| `design/life-cycles.md`, section 7 | Ways of living through the year |
| `design/body.md`, section 12 | Builds this should allow |
| `design/presets.md`, section 3 | The twenty preset species |

| Test | Passes when |
|---|---|
| It can be written | The whole behaviour is a program in the existing words, with nothing left as "and then it somehow" |
| It fits | It is within the allowance for a brain, and small enough to read on a phone |
| It does what it says | Run in the stand-in, the behaviour is there to be seen and counted: the sentinel calls and the others hide, the hoard is filled and later eaten, the pack takes prey none of them could alone |
| It pays somewhere | In at least one setting it does better than the plain version of the same species |

Where a behaviour cannot be written, the pass records exactly what is missing, and the choice is to add the smallest mechanic that makes it possible or to stop promising it. `design/neural-blocks.md` section 13 already keeps a list of what the vocabulary cannot say.

**What it leaves behind.** A verdict for every part and every promised behaviour; the changes to the lists; and a library of the written behaviours. The library is used three more times: as tests the real simulation has to pass, as the preset species, and as examples for the game to teach from.

**What it does not do.** It sets no costs and tunes no numbers. A part can pass while being too strong or too weak, as long as there is somewhere it is the right choice.

### The balance pass

"Balanced" is broken into tests that can each pass or fail.

| Test | Passes when |
|---|---|
| Viable | Every option appears in at least one of the best designs somewhere |
| Not mandatory | No option appears in every winner |
| A real choice | Swapping it for another changes how a winner does in at least one habitat |
| Different winners | Each habitat's best design is best at home and worse away |
| Counters | Any winner can be beaten by some other design, when they share food or in another place |
| Skill shows | A searched design clearly beats a plain one, and a sensible hand-made one gets most of the way there |
| Lasting | A mixed world keeps several species going for a long time |
| Open | Whatever a settled world holds, some different design can establish itself in it |
| Turning over | As newcomers are added one after another, no design stays on top and the world does not collapse to one |

The last three matter most. Balance in the game moves: what does well depends on which other species are around and how they are doing. A world with no hunters fills with grazers, and that is an opening for a hunter. So the lab's main test, once it can search hunters as well as grazers, is to fill a world with the current winners, search for a newcomer that can get in, add it and repeat. The game should also show players where the openings are.

**The balance lab** (`design/checks/balancelab.js`) runs these.

- **Every choice is data.** Body options, behaviour kits and breeding rules are one list the lab combines under the budget. A new part or word is added once and is then tested everywhere. For bodies that list is the species sheet of area 13, so the lab, the drawing and the designer cannot drift apart.
- **A search plays the adversary.** In each habitat it breeds designs toward whatever does best there, as a player trying to break the game would. The earlier audits compared builds picked by hand, which is how they missed things.
- **A report gives verdicts:** who wins where on maps the search has not seen, how often each option is chosen, and whether winners hold their home against visitors.
- **A knockout test** changes one choice at a time in a winner, to show which choices matter and which make no difference.
- **The lab checks itself first:** that hunters are present for the whole run, that a blind design does badly, that removing hunters helps, and how much the same design varies from map to map. A difference smaller than that variation is not reported as a finding.
- **Results are kept** in `design/checks/balance/`, and the lab is re-run whenever a rule or a number changes, so a fix that breaks something else shows up.

**What a simulation cannot test** is whether any of it is fun, readable or satisfying. That is tested with people: mockups to react to in each area, a playable test tank with friends as the first thing built, and in the first season a record of which options get used and how species die.

**First run, 15 November, scored by head count.** Six habitat patches, about a hundred designs searched in each, grazers only. The lab's checks on itself pass. Scores run from 0 to about 2, and differences under about 0.15 are noise.

| Test | Result |
|---|---|
| Not mandatory | **Fails.** The smallest size and the fat-storing inside are in every winner. Any larger size costs 0.5 to 1.6. A large gut does as well as fat in two habitats; every other inside costs 0.3 to 1.0 |
| Viable | **Fails.** Never in a best design anywhere: every size but the smallest, the other three insides, the sting, eyes on stalks, paddles, and not laying when food is thin |
| A real choice | **Mixed.** Size, limbs and the inside matter in all six habitats; a nose and the response to threat in four; shape, eyes and shell in three. Sting, camouflage, false eyes and egg size changed nothing anywhere |
| Different winners | **Fails on land.** Each habitat's winner is the best or near it at home in five of six, but one design is as good as the best in five of the six habitats. Only water forces a different body |
| Counters | **Partly.** A winner holds its home against visiting winners in 24 of 30 pairings. Where two designs share one food, one usually starves the other out completely |
| Skill shows | **Yes.** Winners score about twice the plain design |
| Lasting | Not tested by the lab yet |

What it found:

- **Small wins everywhere, because a plant feeds a small animal as much as a large one.** Upkeep rises with size, so a large body needs about five and a half times the meals of a small one. With no hunters at all, in the meadow, small scored 1.74, plain 1.20 and large 0.56. A large body needs something only it can do: bigger bites, foods it alone can reach, or winning food from others.
- **Storing fat beats every other inside,** as the body audit suspected.
- **Spikes won everywhere at first, for a bad reason.** Test hunters bit spiked prey until they died, which left the habitat with no hunters for most of the run. With dead hunters replaced, spikes were in one winner of six. Hunters need a way to notice spikes and leave them alone, and a species that kills off its hunters is a question for the shared world.
- **The earlier build tables were wrong about what wins.** Their hunters starved about a quarter of the way through each run, because they gained nothing from a kill. Those tables show how hard each build is to catch early on, and say nothing about food or breeding. Large and armoured builds looked dominant there; large builds do worst here. The affected tables in `body.md`, `environment.md` and `world.md` now carry a correction.

**Second run, 15 November, scored by total life.** The same search, with the score you chose, counting weight two ways and then changing one body rule.

| Score | Sizes that win | Inside that wins | One design is near the best in |
|---|---|---|---|
| Head count (first run) | The smallest only | Fat storage, in every winner | 5 of 6 habitats |
| Total life, weight as it is | Large and largest only | A large gut, in 5 of 6 | 5 of 6 |
| Total life, weight counted by appetite | Plain, large and largest; never small | A large gut, in every winner | 4 of 6 |
| Total life, weight as it is, and every unit of weight costs the same to run | Plain and large; never the smallest or the largest | A large gut, in 4 of 6 | 4 of 6 |

- **How the score counts weight decides which body wins.** Count heads and the smallest wins. Count weight and the largest wins, because the same food carries more weight as fewer, larger animals.
- **Decided: weight is counted as it is.** Counting by appetite, or by food eaten, would be even-handed on size, but it would not reward a species for being efficient, and carrying more life on the same food is part of doing well. So the body rules, not the score, have to make both a swarm of small animals and a few large ones worth playing.
- **Why a swarm cannot win today.** In the stand-in a large body burns less food for each unit of weight, so the same food carries about 1.8 times the weight as a few of the largest animals as it does as many of the smallest. When every unit of weight costs the same to run, the largest size stops winning, and plain and large sizes share the habitats. The smallest still never wins: all three test hunters can eat it, and it breeds no faster than anything else.
- **Three failures do not depend on the score.** One inside wins everywhere, whichever it is. Small bodies have no place once heads are not counted. One design is near the best in most habitats.

Limits of the lab today:

- **Its hunters are a fixed cast, replaced when they die,** so nothing a design does to its hunters lasts. Spikes won everywhere while hunters could be killed off and nowhere once they were replaced. The truth is between the two, and needs hunters that are designs as well.
- **A run is 5,000 beats,** so the lasting half of total life is barely tested, and fading is not in it.
- **Food regrows at a fixed rate,** so holding back from laying cannot pay. The root-stock rule from the world design has to go in.
- **It searches grazers only,** against three fixed hunters, with four behaviour choices out of a vocabulary of about a hundred words.
- **It runs the six habitat patches,** not the eight biomes, and about a hundred designs a habitat is a thin search.

Two faults in the stand-in were found on 15 November while testing size (`design/body.md`, section 4): a plant was one meal for a body of any size, and the lab capped a species at 30 animals. Both have switches in the lab (`LEFTOVERS=1`, `CAP=90`), and neither is yet the default, so the runs above were made with the faults in.

Next for the lab: make those two fixes the default and re-run the search with the size rules from the body design; the root-stock food rule; hunter designs searched against the grazer winners and back again; many more behaviour choices; long runs of a mixed world for the "lasting" test; then the fixes to size, the inside, sting and camouflage, each re-run through it.

**Area 13: one species sheet, fitted both ways.** Added on 16 November. The comparison in `design/body.md` section 15 found that the body designer as written can make four of the sixteen creatures in the paper study, and that the kit draws things the rules have no place for. The owner's answer was that it cuts both ways: the look has to be able to show every body part that is designed, and the parts and choices have to be shown clearly and be flexible enough to make creatures that look far beyond the presets.

*The sheet.* A species is one piece of data. The simulation reads it for what the body can do and what it costs, the kit reads it to draw the body, and the designer's screens write it. A part cannot exist in one of the three and not in the others. It has three kinds of line:

| Kind | What is in it | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Choices that mean something | Size, shape, the inside, coverings, senses, limbs and movers, weapons, things for carrying, giving and signalling, and how each form differs from the base | The budget |
| Choices that change only the look | The head and its size; the tail; how the body is cut; the curve of the spine and the resting pose; the face; the style of each covering; pattern; hues | Free |
| Never chosen | The steps of lightness between parts that touch; where the eyes sit; shadows; the gait that follows from the limbs; the posture every body shares | Worked out from the rest |

*The rules of fit.*

1. **Every part in the rules has an entry in the kit.** A part is not finished until it is drawn.
2. **Every entry in the kit belongs to a part in the rules, or is on the list of look-only choices.**
3. **Nothing is drawn that promises what the body cannot do.** Wings mean a mover; a club on a tail means a weapon.
4. **Every choice that means something can be seen.** It changes the outline, or a mark that is still there when the creature is a centimetre long, so a player can read a stranger's design by looking.
5. **Look-only choices are wide, and continuous where they can be:** sizes, lengths and where a part sits along the body are slid, not picked from three. This is where creatures that look nothing like the presets come from.
6. **Any combination draws.** No part hides another completely, eyes stay inside the outline, and parts that touch differ in lightness.
7. **Every body wears every state and bearing and does every act,** because states are rows of numbers for a posture every body has.
8. **A form is the base sheet and its differences,** so the forms of one species look like one family, and the more one differs the more it costs.

*What the area produces.*

- The sheet, written as one data file that the balance lab, the kit and the designer mockup all read.
- A table of cover in both directions: each part in the rules against its entry in the kit, and each entry in the kit against its part or its place among the look-only choices. The gaps known today are in `design/body.md` section 15: the designer has no head, tail, cut of body, curve, face or covering style, and the kit has wings, a body that moves without limbs, arms, a thing worn, a club of a tail and segments, which the rules do not.
- A decision on each of those six. Proposed, for the owner to confirm when the area is worked: wings are a mover for short bursts over land; a body wave is a mover that is slow on open ground and quick in water and reeds; arms are a grip; a club is a weapon at the rear; rounds are how a long body is cut and cost nothing; a thing worn is how camouflage for one ground is drawn, and found objects wait for a later version.
- The kit extended: whatever the rules have and the kit lacks is drawn, and the look-only choices become numbers a player can set.
- The body designer as a concept test on a phone, drawing with the kit itself, with a step for looks after the step for shape.
- `design/body.md` taken to draft 3 with all of this folded in.

*The checks.* Scripts in `design/checks/`, and people.

| Check | Passes when |
|---|---|
| Cover, both ways | No part is without a drawing, and no drawing is without a part or a place on the look-only list |
| The presets fit | All sixteen creatures in the study and all twenty preset species can be written as sheets |
| Seen | For every part that means something, the outline or a mark differs with and without it at one centimetre, by more than a set amount; no two parts are closer to each other than that |
| Any combination | Two hundred sheets made at random draw with no fault: nothing fully hidden, eyes inside, contrast between touching parts, limbs attached |
| Every state and act | The same random sheets wear all twelve states and eight bearings and walk, feed, drink, sleep and startle without breaking |
| Variety | An independent reviewer, shown a hundred random creatures at phone size, can tell any two apart, finds them appealing, and finds most of them unlike any preset |
| A person | Someone who has not seen it makes a creature they like in five minutes on a phone, and can say what each of its parts is for |

*When.* Next, before the screens, because the body designer is one of the two central screens, and before the balance pass, because the lab's list of choices has to be final before numbers are set.

**How the interface gets from mockups to release quality.** The mockups made so far are concept tests. Each answers whether an idea works at all, and none of them is an interface design. The first one, the behaviour editor, was judged on 15 November to work as a concept and to be nowhere near a finished interface. The interface itself is designed in stages, and each stage is reviewed before the next begins.

1. **Concepts.** Rough and interactive, one question each. This is where the mockups are now.
2. **Flows.** Every screen and the paths between them as plain wireframes at phone size, with real content, before any styling. The wireframes settle what each screen is for and what it says. They are not layouts to be dressed. The UI pass reads them for words and order.
3. **A style system.** The paper rulebook: type, colour, spacing, shapes, shadows, motion and sound, and every component drawn in every state it can be in. It goes through the UI pass.
4. **Key screens at full quality,** a few at a time, in several versions set side by side, each batch through the UI pass. This stage starts again from what each moment should feel like and do, with careful and deliberate thought about the design. It does not lean on the wireframes for layout.
5. **Tested on real phones** by two or three friends who have not seen it, then revised.
6. **Only then built** as the game's own components.

**The UI pass.** Asked for on 16 November. It is to the interface what the review rounds were to the look and the mechanics pass is to the rules: nothing about the interface is called done on the main thread's word alone.

- **Who.** Independent reviewers, each a fresh Fable 5.2 agent that has not seen how the screen was made. No other model is used. Each is given the screens at phone size, as pictures and as a page it can try, with one line on what each screen is for.
- **Several at once, each with a different eye:** someone opening the game for the first time; a thumb on a small phone; reading order, type and contrast; whether it feels handmade and is still easy to read; whether depth is revealed a little at a time; whether it is consistent with the other screens.
- **What each returns.**
  1. Comments, worst first, each tied to a place on a screen.
  2. Small changes, each specific enough to make without asking: what, where, and to what.
  3. Other ideas: at least two different layouts or designs for the screen's main problem, described well enough to build. These are wanted even when the screen is acceptable.
  4. A score for each screen against a beautiful shipped game, where 5 is a competent prototype.
- **What the main thread does.** Every proposal is accepted, rejected or tried side by side, and the choice is written in a table with its reason. An accepted alternative is built as a second version beside the first, and the next round judges both. A reviewer is told what was rejected before and why, so it is not proposed again without a new argument.
- **Rounds.** At least three for each group of screens. It stops when two rounds running bring no serious comment and every screen scores 7 or more, or when the reviewers agree on a ceiling and name what would raise it.
- **When.** On the flows, for words and order only. On the style system. On each batch of key screens. On the click-through as a whole. Again in the build, on the real components, in phases 3 and 5.
- **Records.** Each report is kept in `design/reviews/` with the round's table of what was accepted and rejected.
- **What it cannot judge.** Reviewers see pictures and a page in a desktop browser at phone size. How it feels in the hand is for the friends who try it on real phones, which stays a separate step.

**Scoped for area 8: unlocks that widen play.** Ideas to design, not decisions.

- **Kits by way of life.** Parts and words come in small sets that suggest a style, such as a stalker's set, a swarm's set or a water set, so that an unlock is an invitation to try something.
- **Earned by doing something new.** A first hunter, a first species to winter in the Highlands or a first colony unlocks what the next step in that direction needs. Repeating the same design unlocks nothing more.
- **Pointed at the world's openings.** When a biome has no hunters or a food is going uneaten, the next offer leans that way.
- **Variety, not power,** as already decided: the budget stays fixed.

**Scoped for area 9: talking with your creature.** An idea to design. The player taps a few buttons to talk to the name-bearer, and it answers in character. Four things about it are decided: the interactions are a fixed set, the model cannot change the game, the two kinds of memory are kept apart, and memories are stored with the species.

- **What it draws on:** a personality that follows from the species' design, its mood and needs now, and memories: its own, its line's, and what others of its kind have been through.
- **What it is for:** affection, and finding out what happened in the world and what is troubling the species. The player can ask it to do things or give it things, and it may agree or refuse.
- **The model cannot change the game.** The simulation decides whether the creature agrees, from its real moods and instincts, and applies any effect itself. The model only puts the outcome into words, so talking cannot be used to cheat and the model cannot be talked into anything.
- **A fixed set of interactions, not open typing:** a short list of things to say, ask, request and give. It is cheaper and safer, and it suits a phone.
- **Two memories, kept apart.** Species memory is the record of game events and states: what happened to the line and its kind, written only by the simulation. Conversation memory is what the player and the creature have said to each other. The model can read both and add only to the second.
- **Where memories live:** on the server, with the species. How much is kept, what is forgotten and what an heir receives are still to design.
- **How it would run:** a small model on the game's own server is not practical. A server at this price has no graphics card, and a model small enough to run on its processor would be slow, would compete with the simulation, and would write poorly. A cheap hosted model with a daily limit for each player is practical: each reply is short and built from a few facts. The provider, the price and the speed are to be checked when it is designed.
- **It must be optional:** nothing in the game depends on it.
- **Five talks a day for each player** (decided), shown as pips beside the Talk button and refilled overnight.
- **Running out is shown in the game's own language, never as a limit or a credit** (decided): the named one has said what it has to say for today. It still answers a stroke or food with sounds and poses, the Talk button shows it settling down, and it turns back to its own life. The same thing happens if the service is down. Its place in the simulation is untouched, because the model cannot change the game.
- **Every call is the same bounded size,** so the cost has a ceiling: the standing instructions, the species' personality sheet, the last twenty or so events from the species memory, the named line's memories, its state now, and the last few exchanges. About 2,500 tokens in and a reply of a sentence or two, allowed up to 300 tokens out, with the model's extra reasoning turned off.

**The most it can cost.** Twenty players, each using all five talks every day, is 100 calls a day. With one more call a day for each player to shorten their conversation memory, and a personality sheet for each new species, that is about 125 calls a day, or 3,750 a month.

- **Twenty talks a day each,** four times the proposal, would be about $5 a month on the cheapest model.
- **Two guards on the bill:** the server counts talks for each player and refuses the call past the limit, and the provider account carries a monthly spending limit, so that a fault cannot run it up.
- **Not measured:** how quickly a reply comes back, and whether the smallest model writes well enough in a creature's voice. Both need a trial before this is built.

## 9. Building, once the design is closed

The order from draft 1 stands, with the designers first. The technology is the one the exploration report recommended and nobody has yet measured: one process on a small server that runs the world, sends updates and keeps a small database; a web client that can be put on a phone's home screen; and one simulation, written once and used by the server, the test tank and the balance lab.

**Three things every phase rests on.**

- **One simulation.** It is exact and repeatable, so the same design behaves in the tank as in the world, and the lab's tests are the game's tests.

- **Checks that cannot be weakened.** The audits in `design/checks/` and the library of behaviours from the mechanics pass become the simulation's tests. The look is checked from screenshots by a reviewer who did not make it, as in the paper study. Things only people can judge are tried with friends at the end of phases 3, 5 and 7.

## 10. Risks carried into the design

| Risk | Where it is dealt with |
|---|---|
| Hunters and prey cannot both persist, so a player's hunter species may be unable to last | Area 4 found no fix in the stand-in. To be tested first when the real simulation exists, in phase 2; respawning covers presets only |
| A part or a word does nothing, or is never worth choosing | The mechanics pass, part by part, before any number is tuned |
| A behaviour the design promises cannot be built from the vocabulary, or only by us and not by a player on a phone | The mechanics pass writes each one out in full; phase 3 has a friend build one |
| One build wins everywhere | Areas 1, 4 and 5, then the balance pass |
| Friends cannot build behaviours on a phone | Area 8 mockups, then phase 3 |
| The interface is judged only by whoever made it | The UI pass: independent reviewers over several rounds, with every proposal answered |
| A player makes a body the kit cannot draw, or one that looks like every other | Area 13: the checks on random sheets and on variety |
| A part looks as if it does something it does not, or does something that cannot be seen | Area 13: the rules of fit |
| A rich world is too much for a small server or a phone | Area 1 sets the size; measured in phases 2 and 3 |
| States cannot be read when a creature is small | Known from the second review. The art pass, with the rule that a state must read at a centimetre |
| Paper drawn in code stops short of beautiful | The reviewers put its ceiling near 8 out of 10. Scanned paper and photographed edges are decided at the start of the art pass |
| A test is broken and its result is taken as a finding | The balance lab checks itself before each run (section 3) |
| The design grows without end | Each area has a "done when", and area 15 does not start until the others are closed. The mockup stage proves systems and stops |
