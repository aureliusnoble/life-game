# Social organisation: building blocks for many kinds of society

Draft 3, November 2026. Design area 3 in `design/plan.md`. A proposal on paper: nothing in it has been run, and balance is left to the end.

## 1. The aim

There is no menu of societies to pick from. There is a small set of blocks, and a pack, a harem, a hive or something with no counterpart in nature is what a player gets by combining them. Two things have to hold:

- **Breadth:** the blocks reach as much of the range of real societies as possible, and beyond it.
- **Reasons:** every way of living together buys something in the world and costs something. Otherwise it is decoration.

Most of the blocks are already in the behaviour vocabulary (`design/neural-blocks.md`). Section 8 lists the few that are new.

## 2. A society is seven answers

| Question | From | To |
|---|---|---|
| Who breeds? | Every adult | One individual |
| Who mates with whom? | Anyone, once | One partner for life |
| Who looks after the young? | No one | Everyone |
| Who stays together? | No one | A swarm of thousands |
| Who belongs? | No groups at all | Closed colonies that fight each other |
| Who does what? | All alike | Castes with their own bodies |
| Who gives way? | No one | A strict order |

Each answer is built from the blocks in section 3, and any combination is allowed. A wolf pack and a beehive differ on most of the seven; many interesting designs will be mixtures that no animal uses.

## 3. The blocks

### 3.1 Knowing who is who

An instinct can only treat others differently if it can tell them apart.

| A creature can tell | How | Status |
|---|---|---|
| My kind from others | Always | Exists |
| Female from male; young, adult and old | The bodies differ | Exists; "old" is new in the life-cycles draft |
| Caste | The bodies differ | Exists, as a Born with mood |
| One particular individual: my mate, my parent, my young, my rival | A Bond mood | Exists, tested |
| Familiar from stranger | The Learn function | Exists, on paper |
| What another is doing or announcing | Its display, call or mark | Exists |
| The biggest, smallest, weakest or lone one | Selectors | Exists |
| Bigger or smaller than me; older or younger than me, among my kind | A comparison | **New** |
| Of my home, or of another home of my kind | Home scent (3.2) | **New** |

### 3.2 Belonging: homes

This is the block that societies with more than one group need.

- **Home scent.** Every creature carries the scent of the home where it hatched. Its marks carry it too. "Of my home" and "not of my home" are things an instinct can notice in its own kind.
- **A home is a Place.** A creature that digs or builds and then lays has founded one. Whatever hatches there belongs to it.
- **Leaving.** Instincts decide who leaves and when: all the young, one sex, only those born as founders, or no one.
- **Joining.** A creature that is tended or fed at a home for long enough takes on its scent. That covers adoption, and it also means stolen eggs hatch as loyal members of the home that stole them.
- **Many homes.** A species can treat its other homes as friends, as strangers to avoid, or as enemies. That is one instinct, and it decides a great deal (section 7).

### 3.3 Breeding rules

Set for the species in the life-cycle settings (`design/life-cycles.md`), and shaped by instincts.

| Block | What it does | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Way of breeding | Alone, in pairs, or one breeder with helpers | Decided: chosen per species |
| Brood mix | What share of eggs become each sex and each caste, with up to two rules such as "more founders in autumn" or "more soldiers after an attack" | **New** |
| Who may lay | An instinct with conditions. "Lay, not when I smell the Queen mark" keeps everyone but the queen from laying, for as long as she lives | Exists |
| Mating | The mate act, which needs both to want it. What makes one willing is its own instincts: a display, a gift of food, a held territory, a Bond | Exists |
| Keeps what it was given | Mates once and lays for life | **New** |
| What breeding costs the parent | Nothing; the mother dies after laying; the father dies after mating; the father is eaten | **New** as settings; the last is an instinct |
| Young forms | A young body that differs from the adult's | Decided: in the first version |

### 3.4 Roles

Five ways for members of one species to do different jobs. They can be mixed.

| Way | How | Example |
|---|---|---|
| By birth | A Born with mood, with body parts tied to it | Workers, soldiers, a queen |
| By age | "Only when young", "only when old" | Young adults nurse, older ones forage, elders guard |
| By sex | "Only when female" | Males guard while females feed |
| By signal | A creature takes a job by showing it, and others hold off while they can see it shown | One sentinel at a time; one leader at a time |
| By need | A Count, or "few of my home near me showing X" | More foragers when the larder is low |

One addition makes roles that are taken up later in life possible:

- **For life (new mood function).** Once on, the mood never fades, and the creature grows into another form over a few days (section 9). A worker can become a queen when the old one dies. The biggest of a group can change sex. A home under attack can turn some of its young into soldiers. Founders can grow fins or wide feet to cross to new ground.

### 3.5 Giving and sharing

All existing: grab and drop, give, tend, dig and build, and a Place to bring things back to.

- **Feeding others:** the young, the breeder, the guards. This is what lets some members never forage.
- **Passing food along:** "give" shares food already eaten, so a forager can come home and feed several.
- **Stores:** a larder at the home for winter.
- **Carrying the young or the eggs:** by either parent, or by anyone.

### 3.6 Acting together

All existing: calls, marks and displays; Catching moods; Count; "go along" and "go the same way".

- **Recruiting:** a call or a trail that brings others to food, or to a fight.
- **Alarm:** one sees, all flee.
- **Quorum:** "only when many of my home are near" before attacking, moving home or spawning.
- **Following a leader:** go the same way as the oldest, or as the one showing a Lead display.
- **Tradition:** the young follow elders to a place and remember it. Routes, water holes and nesting grounds then pass down the generations for as long as the generations overlap. This is how a species comes to "know" things its designer never told it.

### 3.7 Rank and rivalry

Built from displays and one mood; nothing new.

- **Contests:** two rivals show a threat. The smaller or older-weaker one's Beaten mood rises, and while Beaten it keeps apart and does not mate or lay.
- **Rank by size or by age:** using the new comparisons, "give way to my kind bigger than me" at food.
- **Taking over:** a challenger that beats the breeder drives it out.
- **Policing:** "eat an egg that does not smell of the Queen".
- **Driving out:** the young are chased off when they come of age.

### 3.8 Space

All existing: Place, marks, dig, build.

- **Territory:** mark the edges; chase strangers of my kind inside them.
- **Nest, burrow, hive:** dug or built, and defended.
- **Range without borders:** stay near a Place, tolerate neighbours.
- **No home:** nomads that camp where they stop.
- **Several homes:** a main nest and outposts.

## 4. Why live together: what it buys and what it costs

Each row needs its mechanism to exist in the world, or the society built on it is filler.

| What a group buys | Through | In the world today? |
|---|---|---|
| Earlier warning | Any member's alarm reaches all | Yes |
| Safety in numbers | A hunter takes one at a time, and is torn between targets | Yes |
| Fighting back | Many small bites hurt a hunter that one could not | Yes, and too strong |
| Bigger prey | Several biters; clingers that slow the prey | Partly: needs a rule for wounding prey larger than yourself |
| Finding food sooner | Recruiting calls and trails | Yes |
| Specialised bodies and brains | A guard and a forager, each built for one job and cheaper than an all-rounder | Needs forms (section 9) |
| More young surviving | Tending speeds hatching and growth; guards keep thieves off | Yes |
| Getting through winter | A shared larder; huddling for warmth | Larder yes; warmth is proposed |
| Knowing the land | Tradition | On paper |

| What a group costs | Through |
|---|---|
| Food runs out near home | Members travel farther for every meal |
| Easy to find | A crowd is loud, visible and leaves scent |
| One point of failure | A species with one breeder can lose it |
| Rivals inside | Contests, stolen matings, idle members |
| Brain budget | Every social instinct is one not spent on something else |

## 6. Mothers, fathers and others

The strategies you listed, and their neighbours, with what each trades.

| Strategy | How it is built | The trade |
|---|---|---|
| The mother dies when she lays | Setting; one huge brood | Everything goes into the eggs; nothing is left to guard them |
| The father dies after mating | Setting, with a cheap, short-lived male body | Males cost almost nothing to make |
| The father is eaten | "After mating, bite my mate" | The mother's next brood is paid for by him |
| Many males to one female | Brood mix heavy on males; she lays for each | Plenty of carers and guards; many mouths that lay nothing |
| One male to many females | Contests between males; brood mix or driving out handles the spare males | The best male fathers most; most males never breed |
| One queen at a time | Queen mark and For life | Very efficient; one death can end it |
| Several queens | Tolerated marks | Safer, and each queen gets less |
| The father carries the eggs | Grab and carry, only when male | The mother is free to feed and lay again |
| Both tend | Bond, then take turns by signal | High survival; two adults tied to one nest |
| Helpers | The young stay | A bigger family; fewer new families |
| A nursery | A few tend everyone's young | The rest are freed to feed; one raid takes the lot |
| Someone else raises them | Lay in another's nest | No cost of care; depends on a host being there |

## 7. Many colonies

Once a species has more than one home, how its homes treat each other is a design choice with large effects.

| Stance toward other homes of my kind | What follows |
|---|---|
| Enemies | Homes space themselves out and each guards its food, which limits overgrazing. Lives are spent on border fights |
| Avoid | Spacing without the fighting, and no help in a crisis |
| Friends | Homes sit close and share. The species can become very dense in one place, and can eat it bare |
| Mother and daughters | Friendly to homes founded from mine, hostile to the rest: needs a scent that founders carry with them |
| Raid | Other homes are a source of eggs and stores |

How new homes start is the other choice: a lone founder, a founder with a retinue, the old breeder leaving with half the home, or a fight that splits it.

## 9. Forms: castes, sexes and stages as separate designs

A proposal that replaces "every individual runs the same brain".

**A species is made of forms.** A form is one kind of individual: a sex, a life stage or a caste. Each form has its own body and its own page of instincts and moods. A first species has one form. Young forms and two sexes, both already decided, are forms too, so this is one idea and not three.

### What forms share, and what they do not

| Shared by the whole species | Separate for each form |
|---|---|
| Being one kind, and the home scent | The body |
| The named sounds, scents and displays, so that forms understand each other | Instincts and moods |
| The life-cycle settings | What it costs |
| A common page of instincts, which a form can take or leave | |

A form that leaves the common page can be very simple. A soldier that never forages needs no feeding instincts, and has to be fed by the others.

### The budget

- **Each form is its own design under the same combined allowance for body and brain.** A soldier can be a large body with a three-line brain. A scout can be a small body with senses and memory. A breeder can be little more than an egg organ.
- **That is the reason to specialise:** a simple-minded specialist is cheaper to make and run than an all-rounder that can do everything.
- **The price** is that specialists are helpless alone, and that each form is another design to get right. Whether each extra form should also cost something outright is left for the balance pass.

### When a form is decided, and by whom

| When | Who decides | How | Like |
|---|---|---|---|
| At laying | The one that lays | Brood mix: set shares, with rules tied to her moods, the season, or whether the egg was fathered | Most ants and bees |
| While young | Those that raise it | A For life mood in the young, raised by how it is fed and tended | A queen made by feeding |
| While young | The young itself | A For life mood raised by what it senses: crowding, an alarm scent at home, the season | Soldiers raised after an attack |
| As an adult | Events | A For life mood raised when something changes: the breeder's scent fades, it becomes the biggest, it is crowded | A new queen, sex change, a wandering phase |
| Any time, and reversible | The individual | By age, by signal or by need (section 3.4). No change of body | Sentinels, nurses that become foragers |

### Keeping the shares right

Three ways a designer can steer how many of each form there are. They can be combined.

- **Set shares:** so many workers, soldiers and founders in every brood.
- **The layer responds:** her moods are raised by what reaches her. An alarm scent carried home makes her lay more soldiers; a full larder, more founders.
- **Each form limits itself:** a young one is less likely to become a soldier the more soldiers it can smell. The numbers then settle by themselves, and recover after losses.

### Keeping it learnable

- **Forms are advanced play.** A first species has one, and a second form is an unlock.
- **A limit on forms** per species, perhaps four or five, keeps a design readable.
- **Templates:** a few working societies to open up and change will teach more than any description.

## 10. A wider survey: what the blocks could not reach

This is from general knowledge of animal societies, not from new research. I went through the stranger corners of how organisms live together and asked of each whether the blocks could build it.

**Already reachable,** though not listed in section 5: partners that return favours; alliances of unrelated adults; markets between species such as cleaners and their clients; false alarms used to steal food; choosing a new home by scouts and a quorum; kidnapping young to raise as helpers; a breeder that slips into another colony and takes it over; siblings that kill each other in the nest; eggs laid as food for the young; rival male types that beat each other in a circle, so that none stays on top; and a solitary species that turns into a marching swarm when crowded.

**Not reachable, with what it would take:**

| What organisms do | Why the blocks cannot | What it would take | Add? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Breed by dividing in two, as flatworms and anemones do | Every way of breeding goes through an egg | A fourth way of breeding: splitting into two small adults | Yes |
| Every individual is both sexes, and any two can mate | Pairs need two sexes | A fifth way of breeding | Yes |
| Breed alone in summer and in pairs in autumn; or unfathered eggs become males | One way of breeding per species | A brood mix rule that depends on whether the egg was fathered | Yes |
| Lay eggs on a living host, which the young then eat | An egg stays where it is laid | "Lay" on what it is touching or carrying | Yes |
| Keep the eggs inside until they hatch | Eggs are always laid | A life-cycle option: a slower mother, safer eggs | Yes |
| Bodies joined into one animal, each part a different form, as in a man-of-war | Clinging is one rider on one carrier | Chains of clinging, and food shared through the join | No: decided against. Attaching and letting go is in instead (section 11) |
| Rafts, bridges and nests made of living bodies | Bodies are not material | Clinging bodies that others can walk on or float on | Later |
| Farming: tending a crop, planting seed | Tending works on eggs, young and other creatures | Tending a plant speeds its growth; a dropped seed can sprout | Yes |
| Telling others where food is without leading them there, as bees do | A display can point only at what is in view | A display that points toward a remembered Place | Yes |
| Joining a friend's fight; attacking what the leader attacks | No way to refer to another's target | "The one it is facing" | Yes |
| Stealing food from another's mouth | Grab takes only things lying loose | Grab from a carrier | Yes |
| Paralysing prey to store it alive for the young | Wounds only slow | Venoms that act on a few feelings every creature has: tired, hurt, dazed | Later |
| Flying or swimming in formation to save effort | Following costs the same as leading | Moving close behind another costs less | Later |
| Traps: pits and snares, built alone or together | Digging only makes cover | Dug pits that slow, and built walls that hold | Later |
| Calls that differ between homes and are learned from parents | Signals are fixed by the design | Learned signals | No: outside this level of mechanics |
| Parasites that live inside a host or take over its behaviour | Nothing acts inside another body or on another's moods | A separate system | No |

The ones marked "Yes" are small, and each opens a family of societies. Decided on 15 November: they go into the vocabulary when area 6 revisits it.

## 11. Attaching and letting go

Decided: bodies joined for good into one animal are out. Creatures that attach to each other and come off again are in, along with defences against it. Clinging is already an act; this section gives it enough rules to be a way of life.

### What attaching is for

| Use | Who attaches to whom | What it gains |
|---|---|---|
| Being carried | Young on a parent; a small form on a large form of its kind; a hitchhiker on another species | Travel for free, across water or ground it could not cross, under the carrier's protection |
| Feeding on the carrier | A parasite | A meal that walks. The carrier is weakened, not killed |
| Cleaning the carrier | A cleaner | The carrier's parasites as food, in return for a service |
| Holding prey | Hunters | Each one slows the prey until another can bite |
| Mating | A small male on a female | It never has to find a mate again |
| Borrowed defence | A soft animal riding on a spiked or stinging one, or carrying one | Protection it did not pay for |
| Being led home | A thief or a raider on a forager | It is carried to the nest or the larder |
| Holding on to the world | Anything, on a rock or a stem | Not swept away by current, tide or wind; somewhere to sleep |

### The rules that make it a contest

- **Where it holds.** An attacher holds the carrier's front, back or flank. Most bodies cannot bite their own back.
- **Load.** Each rider slows the carrier and tires it sooner, and many small riders add up. This is in the stand-in already.
- **Grip against skin.** How well it holds depends on its grip and on the carrier's skin at that spot. No grip holds everywhere.

| Grip | Holds on | Fails on | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Suckers | Smooth skin, shell, wet bodies | Bristles and slime | Slow to attach |
| Hooks | Bristles and rough skin | Shell and slime | Easily scraped off |
| Locking jaws | Anything soft | Shell; hurt by spikes | The mouth is busy, so it cannot bite again |

- **While attached** a creature can feed, sting, clean, give, lay on the carrier, signal, or let go. It cannot steer, and it sees little.
- **Letting go is an instinct like any other:** when full, on smelling its home, when the carrier enters water.
- **The carrier feels it.** "Something is clinging to me" is a new thing to notice, with where it is and whether it is my kind.

### Getting something off

| Defence | How | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| Shake | Sprint and turn hard; weak grips fail | Stamina |
| Roll | A new act. Riders on the back are thrown or crushed | It stops, belly up, for a moment |
| Scrape | Go along a rock, a stem or the edge of a thicket | There has to be one nearby |
| Dive, or leave the water | Riders that cannot breathe there let go | The right limbs, and water |
| Withdraw | Pull into the shell; riders on the soft parts are scraped off | A shell |
| Shed | Drop the tail or the patch of skin being held. The attacker is left holding it | The part has to regrow |
| Be cleaned | Another of its kind, or a cleaner species, removes the riders. A display can ask for it | A partner |
| Skin | Slime, spikes or shell where riders would hold | Budget |
| Keep them off | Keep apart from small strangers; face them; spikes at the rear | Attention |

### What this adds

- **To notice:** something clinging to me.
- **Acts:** roll, shed.
- **Body parts:** three grips; slime as a skin; a tail or skin that can be shed.
- **Feeding without killing:** a small, slow bite from an attached creature takes food from the carrier without wounding it badly.
- **Holding on to the world:** clinging to a rock or a stem.
- **A rule that packs need,** already noted in section 4: an attached creature can wound a carrier of any size.

## 12. What the player sees

- **Homes on the map,** each with its numbers.
- **Who does what:** counts by role, and who is breeding.
- **The named line in a colony** is the breeder's line.
- **Events:** a new home founded, a queen replaced, a border fight, a raid.

## 13. To check in the stand-in, for direction only

1. A sentinel by signal: does the group eat more and lose fewer?
2. A queen mark that stops others laying: does it keep a home from eating its ground bare?
3. A guard caste and a forager caste against the same number of all-rounders.
4. Homes that are enemies against homes that are friends: spacing, and what happens to the food.
5. A pack against prey no lone hunter can take.
6. Riders against a carrier: does each grip fail against the right skin, and does each defence work?

## 14. Answered

Decided on 15 November:

- **All unkind behaviour is allowed:** killing a rival's young, eating a mate, stealing eggs, raiding one's own kind.
- **Forms:** each caste, sex and stage is its own design under the same allowance, sharing only signals, settings and an optional common page (section 9).
- **The additions marked "Yes" in section 10** go in, except bodies joined into one animal.
- **Attaching and letting go** is in, with defences (section 11).
