# Rule cards unlock a programmable pocket ecosystem

The research converges on a design a solo hobbyist working with Claude Code can realistically build: a behaviour editor made of priority-ordered WHEN→DO rule cards grouped into a few mode pages (not a weighted neural graph), soft round critters with faces animated entirely by code, a species lifespan set by specialist enemies that accumulate against successful species (not by a timer), and an all-TypeScript stack whose deterministic simulation runs headless so the agent can test it. No shipped game was found that combines player-designed species, programmable behaviour and a shared persistent ecosystem for a casual audience; the nearest precedent, Microsoft's .NET Terrarium of 2002, required real code. Each neighbouring game stalled on a different leg: Spore on editors that became cosmetic, Creatures and The Bibites on opacity and hand-authoring difficulty, Screeps on a programmer-only audience. The technical side carries the least risk: PixiJS with DOM UI, one Node process with SQLite on a VPS costing roughly €6 a month, delivered as a PWA. Claude Code has a documented record on exactly this web stack, provided the project gives it pass/fail checks it can run and the developer stays in the loop as playtester; Claude at runtime earns its place only for offline, fact-grounded recap narration, at about a dollar a month for 20 players. Two caveats frame everything that follows. Nearly all third-party evidence rests on search-result summaries rather than full-page reads. And the numbers that matter most to this game (tick cost, phone frame rates, lifespan tuning, whether adult non-programmers can author rule cards on a phone) are reasoned estimates that only prototypes will settle.

## Most third-party evidence is snippet-level, so read every number as a lead

All five researchers hit a shared web-search limit and found most sites unreachable from the research sandbox. Two bodies of evidence were read in full: Anthropic's own documentation, engineering posts and price list, and pages hosted on GitHub (library release pages and a few documentation sources), which makes version numbers and release dates the most reliable third-party facts here. Everything else (design post-mortems, academic findings, review digests, vendor pricing, player quotes) comes from search-result summaries, so wording and figures need checking against the linked page before anyone relies on them.

This report keeps three kinds of statement apart. Cited findings carry a link. Inferences are labelled as the researchers' reasoning, as constructions, or as proposals. Items the researchers could only recall from memory are left out, or named explicitly as unverified where they bear on an open question. Where sources conflict on something that touches a recommendation, the conflict is stated. Several topics the brief asked about were not researched at all because the search budget ran out, including idle-game and roguelike run-length design, god-game precedents, notification-fatigue research, and most cute-game post-mortems; silence on those topics is a gap, not a finding.

## Every neighbouring game stalled on a different leg of the concept

Spore's Creature Creator succeeded as what lead designer Chaim Gingold called a "magic crayon", a tool simple to use that still produces something its user values, built on a loop he described as "you do something, you look at what's done, you give and take" ([Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2007-03-12-spores-power-struggle-freedom-vs-beauty.html)). The game around it failed for a reason that bears directly on this project. Soren Johnson's post-mortem locates the root problem in "a philosophical debate about the role of the editor in the game": aesthetic freedom at the expense of gameplay consequence, or mechanics that respond to every choice. Creature parts ended up with gameplay effects that were "unfortunately quite discrete", and in later stages the editors "served a mostly cosmetic role" ([Designer Notes](https://www.designer-notes.com/spore-my-view-of-the-elephant/)). Johnson's own counterfactual, keeping only the biological stages and exploring them in depth, is close to this game's scope. Two further Spore facts matter later. The creator used **two separate limits**, a DNA-point budget and a complexity meter ([Spore Wiki](https://spore.fandom.com/wiki/Creature_Creator)). And its celebrated animation was not animation from nothing: animators authored motion that a research-grade inverse-kinematics solver retargeted to each creature ([Hecker et al.](https://chrishecker.com/Real-time_Motion_Retargeting_to_Highly_Varied_User-Created_Morphologies)).

Creatures coupled a nine-lobe neural network to a simulated biochemistry in which drives, reward and punishment are all chemicals; its creator Steve Grand later said the biochemistry's complexity made behaviour highly unpredictable ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creatures_(video_game_series))). Players report symptoms with no visible cause, such as a Norn whose hunger "just shoots up and is constantly in the red" ([Creatures Caves forum](http://creaturescaves.com/forum.php?thread=6150&view=3)). The researchers infer that attachment came from named, long-lived individuals with visible needs rather than from the neural net, and that lifetime learning is the least transferable piece for a multi-day species contest. No source documenting player attachment to Norns directly was retrieved.

The Bibites offers the closest thing to a literal neural editor: a NEAT-style graph with a default of 33 input and 15 output neurons (a later community guide counts 23 inputs, so the figure is version-dependent), typed hidden nodes and weighted synapses ([Bibites Wiki](https://the-bibites.fandom.com/wiki/Brain)). Its only budget is energy. Each hidden neuron and synapse adds to egg cost and upkeep, and the developer once lowered brain upkeep specifically "to encourage bigger brains" ([Neuron Birth Cost](https://the-bibites.fandom.com/wiki/Neuron_Birth_Cost); [0.4.1 devlog](https://itch.io/devlog/362871/the-bibites-v041.amp)). Hand-authoring is hard: one player needed seven rounds of adjustment before a hand-built brain "started to work mostly as intended" and called the network "basically just another programming language" ([Steam guide](https://steamcommunity.com/sharedfiles/filedetails/?id=3441916076)), and a forum thread is titled "seemingly unreadable brains" ([itch.io](https://itch.io/t/2535360/seemingly-unreadable-brains), title only). The idea most worth borrowing arrived in version 0.6.0: internal organs compete for physical space inside the body, so trade-offs come from geometry rather than a price list ([0.6.0 devlog](https://thebibites.itch.io/the-bibites/devlog/809631/the-bibites-060-organs-and-science)).

Games that let non-programmers program units converge on one interface: a priority-ordered list of condition→action rules, evaluated top-down, stopping at the first match. Final Fantasy XII's team chose gambits because "we didn't want it to be overwhelming" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/why-i-final-fantasy-iv-i-was-key-to-i-ffxii-i-s-ai-driven-gambit-system)); Unicorn Overlord uses the same model, and a reviewer called it "really in-depth without becoming too complex" ([Prima Games](https://primagames.com/gaming/unicorn-overlord-tactics-and-conditions-explained); [Zatu](https://zatu.com/blogs/reviews/unicorn-overload-review)). Gladiabots, the most relevant mobile example, uses a tree of conditions and actions, holds a "Very Positive" Steam rating on roughly 830 to 870 reviews, and still draws a recurring complaint about its learning curve and debugging ([Steam](https://store.steampowered.com/app/871930); [Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.GFX47.Gladiabots&hl=en_US)). At the other extreme, Screeps, a persistent world programmed in real JavaScript, retains programmers only: newcomers describe their code as "a pile of spaghetti", and a veteran suspects churn peaks at world wipes because repeating the same climb is tedious ([Screeps forum](https://screeps.com/forum/topic/1994/what-did-you-find-annoying-hard-when-you-started-out-with-this-game/13); [Future of Screeps](https://screeps.com/forum/post/8357)).

The two named inspirations at the small end of the scale teach opposite lessons. RoboZZle shows how small a deep language can be: a handful of primitive actions, a colour condition attachable to any instruction, and a few short function slots ([RoboZZle Wiki](https://robozzle.com/wiki/Print.aspx?Page=Commands)). The transferable idea is the condition as a modifier on an instruction, which keeps a program a flat row of chips; its recursion-and-stack depth is puzzle material, not standing behaviour for an ecosystem. Lego Mindstorms contributes a warning. NXT-G's icons on parallel "sequence beams" suit short sequences, but parallel beams complicate "how the blocks are run and when the program ends", and critics say large graphical programs "become crowded and unmanageable" ([Brickimedia](https://en.brickimedia.org/wiki/Mindstorms_NXT-G); [FLL team survey](https://fll-pigeons.github.io/gamechangers/AltProgLangs.html), attribution uncertain). Lego's stated reasons for later moving to Scratch-style blocks were not retrieved. The researchers read Mindstorms' real contribution as the build-run-watch loop, whose equivalent here is watching a creature in a test tank immediately after each edit.

Simulation-first evolution games hit a legibility wall. A review digest of Ecosystem reports complaints that evolution "appears random" and that there are "too many factors driving evolution, making it hard for players to track" ([Vaporlens](https://vaporlens.app/app/1133120/ecosystem.md)). The games that read well gate change through a small visible budget. Thrive gives exactly 100 mutation points per editor session and says it "traded some realism" to let players choose their mutations ([Thrive Developer Wiki](https://wiki.revolutionarygamesstudio.com/wiki/Editors_And_Mutations); [Devblog #5](https://www.revolutionarygamesstudio.com/devblog-5-thrive-vs-species)). The board game Evolution caps a species at three traits ([Sprites and Dice](https://spritesanddice.com/reviews/evolution-review/), attribution uncertain) under its designer's rule that an adaptation suited to one situation is poorly suited to another ([Games Precipice](https://www.gamesprecipice.com/dominic-crapuchettes/)). Cell Lab shows a finite "mode table" genome is authorable on a phone and unlocks new cell types through survival challenges; store listings cite 20 modes with 22+ parameters each, though other sources say 15, 40 or 52 modes ([TapTap listing](https://www.taptap.io/app/16414); [Cell Lab Wiki](https://cell-lab.fandom.com/wiki/Genome)). Ultima Online adds a warning for shared worlds: its closed resource loop collapsed in beta because players hoarded outputs, and Raph Koster's lesson is that in-house testing does not predict real player behaviour ([Raph Koster](https://www.raphkoster.com/?p=46439)).

The closest precedent for the whole concept is Microsoft's .NET Terrarium (2002). Developers coded herbivores, carnivores or plants and released them into a peer-to-peer ecosystem to out-survive everyone else's, with statistics compared on a companion site ([Microsoft](https://news.microsoft.com/source/2002/02/25/microsoft-terrarium-game-demonstrates-continuing-developer-enthusiasm-for-net-platform/)). **More than 9,000 developers downloaded it in its first week**, and each creature got 2 to 5 ms of compute per turn before being destroyed for overrunning ([Terrarium 2.0 announcement](https://weblogs.asp.net/bsimser/reintroducing-terrarium-now-with-2-0-goodness/)). It validates the fantasy (release it, walk away, check the standings), and its audience was bounded by requiring real code. **No shipped game was found that combines all three legs for a casual audience.** That finding is provisional: it rests on a single search for recent titles, and several candidates (DarwinBots, Grobots, Framsticks, Screeps Arena) were not researched.

Read together, the neighbours define three requirements, each an inference resting on a documented failure. Body choices must change simulation outcomes continuously, or the editor becomes dress-up (Spore). The player must be able to answer "why did my species die?", which rules out hidden intermediate state the interface cannot show (Creatures, Ecosystem). And authoring must be constrained and scaffolded: slot scarcity delivers balance, learnability and identity at once, and FFXII's License Board shows what happens when a constraint is removable, since characters stopped being differentiated once players could acquire everything ([FF Wiki](https://finalfantasy.fandom.com/wiki/License_Board)).

## Priority rule cards in mode pages fit a phone better than any neural graph

The designer calls the editor "neural", and the heart of the game. The evidence argues against a literal graph of weighted synapses and for a different block language: **two to six named modes, each holding a short priority-ordered list of WHEN→DO cards, where the highest card whose condition is true controls the body**. This is Kodu's structure mapped onto a phone. Kodu is described as "a concurrent rule system derived from contemporary robotics practice" and "a natural state machine language", that is, rules inside states ([MacLaurin, POPL 2011](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1926385.1926413)). No controlled comparison of this hybrid against alternatives with non-programmers was found, so the recommendation is an argument from adjacent evidence, set out below.

### Non-programmers already think in when→do rules

In Pane, Ratanamahatana and Myers' study of how non-programmers describe programs, 54% of statements were event-based ("what should happen when..."), a figure that comes here via a secondary summary and should be confirmed in the original ([McInnes summary](https://allanmcinnes.github.io/2016/05/15/intuitive-functional-requirements); [dblp record](https://dblp.uni-trier.de/rec/journals/ijmms/PaneRM01.html)). Ur and colleagues analysed 67,169 IFTTT programs, ran a 226-participant usability test, and concluded that "inexperienced users can quickly learn to create programs containing multiple triggers or actions" ([CHI 2014](https://www.springerprofessional.de/doi/10.1145/2556288.2557420)). Kodu's when-do language is entirely event-driven, yet its users "express and explore fundamental computer science concepts" with "complex control flow and boolean logic" ([Stolee & Fristoe](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228414070_Expressing_computer_science_concepts_through_Kodu_game_lab)).

The same literature names the traps. Huang and Cakmak found that people interpret trigger-action programs inconsistently because triggers can be states or events and actions can be instantaneous, extended or sustained ([UW HCR Lab](https://hcrlab.cs.washington.edu/publications/huang2015ubicomp)). Brackenbury and colleagues identified ten classes of trigger-action bug and showed in a 153-participant study that their presence made rule behaviour harder to predict ([NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10106413)). A follow-up found users "do not always correctly understand how a sensor/effector works" ([Zancanaro et al.](https://iris.unitn.it/bitstream/11572/356861/1/Zancanaro%20et%20al.%202022-%20HCIS-12-48.pdf)). The researchers' answer is to remove the ambiguity by construction: every WHEN is a state re-evaluated each think tick, every DO is sustained while its card is winning, one-shot events appear as short-lived states ("recently hurt"), and there is exactly one conflict rule, which the interface shows live.

### Drag-and-drop is the documented weak point on touch

Evidence for phones specifically is thin but points one way. A custom soft keyboard for Blockly on touchscreens made block entry faster than drag-and-drop, with the abstract reporting a 71% speed gain, fewer errors and lower frustration ([IEEE Xplore](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/8506557/)). An expert review rated Pocket Code, a phone-first block editor, 1.5 out of 5 for usability and said designing on a phone "considerably limits the ergonomic comfort" ([arXiv 1812.00048](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1812.00048)). ScratchJr cut its vocabulary to about 30 enlarged blocks because Scratch's count was "overwhelming and not necessary" ([DevTech](https://sites.bc.edu/devtech/wp-content/uploads/sites/181/2018/02/EKazakoff-SRCD-.pdf)). A Human Resource Machine player reported hit-detection trouble on an older iPhone where the iPad was fine ([TouchArcade](https://toucharcade.com/community/threads/288605)). Gladiabots' tree editor needs snap-to-grid, layout-only connector nodes and a move-parent-without-children modifier to stay manageable, and a Play Store commenter still calls it "great, but too messy" ([Gladiabots devlog](https://gfx47.itch.io/gladiabots/devlog/137457/1413-changelog-latest-dev-version); [Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.GFX47.Gladiabots&hl=en)).

**No user study was found of any block or rule editor on phone-sized screens with adult non-programmers**; nearly all touch evidence concerns tablets and children. From what exists, the researchers infer an interface with these properties: cards in a single vertical list, where order is priority; tap an empty slot to open a bottom-sheet picker showing only blocks that are legal and unlocked there, which makes syntax errors impossible; drag reserved for one large-target gesture, reordering whole cards, with arrow buttons as an alternative; numbers as three-to-five-step named levels ("near / mid / far") rather than continuous values; icons paired with short words; and exactly two zoom levels, the species overview and one mode's card list. Kodu is the precedent for building rules by stepping through tile choices rather than dragging, since it was designed to "work with just a game controller" ([eSchool News](https://www.eschoolnews.com/research/2010/01/14/microsoft-brings-kids-developer-tool-to-the-pc/)).

### Alternatives fail on readability, tuning or edit cost

The table condenses the researchers' comparison of eleven representations. The failure-mode column draws on the cited sources; the phone-fit and edit columns are the researchers' reasoning, not measurements.

| Representation | Documented failure mode | Fit for a 6-inch portrait screen | Small edit to a living species |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rule cards in mode pages | Rule-order surprises; state-versus-event misreadings ([NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10106413)) | Excellent: a vertical list is the native phone layout | Local: read only the cards above the edit |
| Behaviour tree | Monolithic trees "very hard to extend or refactor" ([arXiv 2008.11906](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2008.11906)) | Poor to fair: needs pan and zoom | Often requires re-reading and re-parenting |
| State machine | Explicit transitions grow as n² ([Halo 3 slides](https://web.cs.wpi.edu/~rich/courses/imgd4000-d09/lectures/halo3.pdf)) | Fair if states are tabs holding rules | A new state needs many transitions |
| Utility scoring | Multiplied scores shrink as considerations are added; one zero vetoes ([Utility Intelligence docs](https://uintel-go.utilityworlds.com/Documentation/UtilityIntelligence/Considerations/)) | Fair: sliders work, many hidden numbers | Every change shifts the balance against all others |
| Weighted sensor→motor graph | Seven rounds of adjustment for one hand-built brain ([Steam guide](https://steamcommunity.com/sharedfiles/filedetails/?id=3441916076)); a thread titled "seemingly unreadable brains" ([itch.io](https://itch.io/t/2535360/seemingly-unreadable-brains)) | Poor: wires are tiny touch targets | Opaque: a weight change touches every path |
| Steering weight mix | "Finding 'right' weight can be challenging"; equilibrium deadlock ([SDU lecture notes](https://imada.sdu.dk/~rolf/Edu/DM842/E17/lec3.pdf)) | Good as parameters of one action only | Coupled: retune everything |
| Imperative blocks or tiny instruction language | Drag-and-drop assembly is slower and more error-prone on touch ([IEEE Xplore](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/8506557/)); slot-limited languages read as puzzles | Poor for scripts; good only for puzzles | Reordering changes semantics |
| Goal-oriented planning | Designers lose direct control of behaviour ([Game AI Pro ch. 4](https://www.gameaipro.com/GameAIPro/GameAIPro_Chapter04_Behavior_Selection_Algorithms.pdf)) | Poor | Plans cannot be predicted |

Three of the options collapse into one family on a phone: a rule list, subsumption layers and a one-level selector tree are all "first applicable item in a priority-ordered list wins". Professional game AI values the same things this audience needs. Damian Isla framed Halo 2's AI problem as "how can you add complexity to a brain while maintaining the transparency of a small system?" ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/programming/gdc-2005-proceeding-handling-complexity-in-the-i-halo-2-i-ai)).

### The six target behaviours need sensors, signals and a latch, not a richer language

The brief lists day/night differences, sexual dimorphism, hunting, packs, mating behaviour and choice of nesting grounds. The researchers sketch how each can be built from rule cards if sensors are rich enough; **these are constructions, not sourced examples, and their learnability is untested.** Day/night and sex become either conditions ("WHEN night", "WHEN I am female") or separate mode pages. Hunting and fleeing are target-selecting sensors ("nearest prey", "weakest prey", "predator in range") plus movement actions. Packs emerge from a few rules per member that exchange signals, with flocking weights hidden inside a single "stay with group" action that exposes one or two sliders, which confines the documented weight-tuning problem. Mating rituals are signal exchanges gated by sex. Nesting grounds are scent marks: leave a mark at a good site, lay eggs when gravid and the mark is sensed. Rituals and parental care need a little memory, which a flag (latch) block and a timer block provide without loops.

Shipping games supply the social primitives. The Bibites gives creatures three coloured pheromone outputs with matching strength and direction inputs, plus a count of visible kin, and draws the trails in the world ([Bibites Wiki: Pheromones](https://the-bibites.fandom.com/wiki/Pheromones)). Gladiabots lets bots write tags that team-mates' conditions read, and picks targets through a filter pipeline rather than variables ([Gladiabots Wiki](https://wiki.gladiabots.com/index.php?title=Advanced)). Cell Lab cells switch mode when a signal substance is high or low ([Cell Lab Wiki](https://cell-lab.fandom.com/wiki/Genome_Editor)). No source evaluates how well non-programmers understand pheromone-style signalling compared with explicit messaging.

Drives belong in the simulation as built-in sensors (hunger, fear, mating urge, fatigue) with thresholds on cards. Full utility-curve authoring is the wrong default because the reason a behaviour lost becomes a number rather than a position in a list; an optional "urgency" modifier that lets a card jump the queue in proportion to a drive can arrive later as an unlock. One caution applies: Will Wright reported that The Sims' early autonomy was "too good", so that almost anything the player did was worse than autopilot ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/games/the-sims/will-wright-says-the-original-sims-ai-was-actually-too-good-almost-anything-the-player-did-was-worse-than-the-sims-running-on-autopilot)). Built-in drives must display mood without solving survival for the player.

### The cheapest explanation is one integer: which card won

Players of Gladiabots and The Bibites single out debugging and readability as the hard part, and professional tooling shows what to copy. CryEngine records agents' decisions because "it is often one bad or wrong decision the agent made some time ago" that caused the visible failure ([CryEngine docs](https://www.cryengine.com/docs/static/engines/cryengine-3/categories/1114113/pages/1048661)). Guerrilla Games describes debugging as having moved from "what" to "why?" and "why not?" ([Guerrilla Games](https://www.guerrilla-games.com/read/out-of-sight-out-of-mind-improving-visualization-of-ai-info)). Gladiabots already shows a selected bot's rules and how they are being evaluated during a match ([cordinc](https://www.cordinc.com/blog/2016/06/gladiabots.html)).

A rule-card brain makes this nearly free, because the full explanation of a decision is the active mode, the index of the winning card and the few sensor values in its condition. The researchers propose four views built on that: a thought bubble above the creature showing the winning card's icons; a "why not" readout on any card that did not fire ("no kin within 6 m; nearest was 9 m"); per-card firing counts with warnings for cards that never fire or are always shadowed by a higher card, which targets the documented rule-interaction bugs directly; and an always-on low-rate trace for a sample of individuals, so a death can be explained afterwards as an autopsy. Sensor ranges, target lines and coloured scent drawn in the world make watching itself diagnostic. No research was found on player-facing explanation views as opposed to developer tools, so their effect on comprehension is an assumption.

### Lists make two-minute edits safe, and slots should gate capacity

The daily check-in depends on small edits to a living species. In a list, a card's effect is bounded by its position, so tweaking a threshold, adding a card, reordering or swapping a block are self-contained; in graphs, trees and weight mixes the meaning is distributed and an edit forces a re-read. That ranking is inference, and no empirical comparison of edit time across representations was found. The researchers' proposed safety net is a draft that shows a human-readable diff before release, a ten-second sandbox preview, one-tap revert, a per-card on/off toggle, and edits that take effect on newborn individuals only. No source describes version history or diffing in any behaviour-authoring game, which makes it a possible differentiator rather than a proven pattern.

Shipped games gate behaviour capacity additively, so old designs stay valid. FFXII sells ten gambit slots at rising cost ([Prima Games](https://primagames.com/eguides/final-fantasy-xii-the-zodiac-age-eguide/zodiac-job-system)), Gladiabots counts only conditions and actions toward its node limit and leaves layout free ([Gladiabots Wiki](https://wiki.gladiabots.com/index.php?title=Advanced)), Screeps meters CPU per tick ([Screeps docs](https://docs.screeps.com/cpu-limit.html)), and Cell Lab unlocks vocabulary through challenges ([namu.moe](https://namu.moe/w/%EC%85%80%EB%9E%A9)). The researchers' inference is to price sensors rather than rules, because perception ("nearest kin", "predator in range") is the server's real cost and the biological trade-off; to charge nothing for organisation; and to stamp every brain with the ruleset version it was authored under, so later balance changes can be migrated or grandfathered. No source discusses migrating player-authored behaviour across language changes.

## Soft critters with faces deliver the most cuteness per hour of solo effort

The designer's worry is that anything more complex than a microbe needs art and animation capacity a solo hobbyist lacks. The research locates the cost cliff precisely, and it sits higher than feared. The ladder below is the tech researcher's synthesis; the two evidence anchors are that top-down limbed animals can be animated entirely by code, and that Spore's side-view creatures could not.

| Rung | What must move | Hand-keyed animation? | Solo-dev cost | Cuteness and variety |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Microbes and cells | Membrane wobble, flagella, drift | None | Lowest; the risk is sameness | Low to medium; hard to bond with an individual |
| 2. Soft critters with faces and attachable features | Squash-and-stretch, hop or waddle, eyes, attachments that lag | None | Low; one body system plus one attachment system covers every part | Highest per unit of effort |
| 3. Top-down limbed animals | Spine curve, stepping legs, tail | None | Medium; gait tuning for arbitrary leg counts, more CPU | Medium-high variety; faces point away from a top-down camera |
| 4. Side-view or 3D articulated animals | Balance, ground contact, gaits per body plan | Yes in Spore's case | Very high; research-grade | Highest in principle, with frequent creepy failures |

Rung 3 is demonstrably small in code. An open-source project animates a fish, a snake and a lizard from one shared distance-constraint chain, with legs solved by FABRIK inverse kinematics ([argonautcode/animal-proc-anim](https://github.com/argonautcode/animal-proc-anim); [technique write-up](https://battesonb-github-io.pages.dev/2024/11/16/procedural-animation)). Rain World shipped commercially with creature motion produced by code and physics, its slugcat body being two physics chunks held at a fixed distance ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/art/video-animating-i-rain-world-i-and-its-many-squishy-stretchy-creatures)). Rung 4 is where Spore needed animator-authored motion plus a robust retargeting solver ([Hecker et al.](https://chrishecker.com/Real-time_Motion_Retargeting_to_Highly_Varied_User-Created_Morphologies)). **The cliff is between rungs 3 and 4, not between microbes and everything else.**

Cuteness maps onto cheap geometry. Glocker and colleagues manipulated infant photos toward and away from the baby schema (round face, high forehead, big eyes, small nose and mouth) and found the high-schema versions were rated cuter and raised caretaking motivation ([Glocker et al. 2009](https://repository.upenn.edu/entities/publication/2bfa14b1-140d-43bc-94ac-6dbffa99194a)); a later paper reports the effect generalises to animal faces ([Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00411/full)). A rung-2 critter whose head is its body, with large low-set eyes, is baby schema by construction. Attachment has a separate source: commentary on the Tamagotchi effect locates the emotional weight "not from graphics or narrative, but from behavior" and in felt responsibility ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamagotchi_effect)). The tech researcher's further list of low-cost charm devices (pupils that track a target, random blinks, squash-and-stretch, attachments that overshoot and settle, pitched chirps, reactions to a poke) is design-practice knowledge that was not verified against sources in this research.

For rendering, the researchers rank layered vector parts with procedural motion as the best balance of responsiveness to the player's design and cost. Each part is a shape with anchor points and continuous parameters (size, stretch, colour, position), written as code or SVG paths, which an agent can author and a human can review as a diff; ears, tails and antennae are short verlet chains anchored to body points. Shader-based distance-field blending is fully continuous and squishy, but even the cheap polynomial smooth-minimum costs a few operations per shape pair at every pixel ([iquilezles.org](https://iquilezles.org/articles/smin)), and the researchers judge full-screen multi-shape evaluation the thing most likely to exhaust a mid-range phone, so it belongs at most in a close-up editor preview. Rive and Spine are runtimes for animation authored in their editors ([Rive docs](https://rive.app/docs/runtimes/web/canvas-vs-webgl)); the researchers judge that their strengths do not transfer to player-assembled bodies whose rig is not known in advance. AI-generated part libraries face the known style-consistency problem, "one great image is easy" while a consistent set is not ([Summer Engine](https://www.summerengine.com/blog/ai-2d-game-asset-generator)); no evidence was gathered on current tools specifically for sprite part sets. Procedural pixel art from mask templates is cheap and agent-friendly ([zfedoran/pixel-sprite-generator](https://github.com/zfedoran/pixel-sprite-generator)) but loses fine design distinctions at low resolution; it suits species portraits for recaps and share images.

Camera and framing follow from the rung. A top-down camera removes gravity, ground contact and facing flips, and suits a spatial ecosystem; drawing faces and eyes toward the viewer on top of top-down bodies restores the face at no extra animation cost. A separate front-facing close-up screen with idle animations and touch reactions can carry the Tamagotchi feeling without changing the world camera. A tide pool, pond, terrarium or garden bed justifies small cute creatures, supports nests, burrows, weather and day/night, and explains a bounded shared world. The feasibility of hundreds of creatures on a phone rests on a level-of-detail plan that is reasoned rather than measured: simulate rigs only for on-screen creatures at close zoom, draw one baked texture per species at mid zoom with separate eye sprites, and draw dots at far zoom. No phone benchmark of any candidate renderer was found.

## A rising enemy clock, one shared budget and harmless absence shape the loop

### Specialist enemies give a soft lifespan that scales with success

The designer wants a natural pressure that ends a species in roughly three to seven real days without a hard cap. The best-grounded mechanism is enemy-mediated negative frequency dependence. In the "kill the winner" model of microbial ecology, abundant types face the heaviest viral pressure, which stops any one type from dominating ([Winter et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2832346)). The Janzen-Connell effect is the plant version: a meta-analysis found survival significantly reduced near conspecifics and at high conspecific density ([Comita et al.](https://digitalcommons.usu.edu/biology_facpub/1551)), and a field experiment found that removing fungal pathogens reduced seedling diversity ([Bagchi et al.](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1038/nature12911)). The mechanism also works in simulation. In Avida, parasites raised host diversity through negative frequency-dependent selection, and coevolved hosts escaped by acquiring more complex functions ([BEACON](https://beacon-center.org/?p=676); [Zaman et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4267771)). In Tierra, parasites, immune hosts and hyper-parasites emerged without being coded ([Ray](https://faculty.cc.gatech.edu/~turk/bio_sim/articles/tierra_thomas_ray.pdf)).

The researchers' proposal built on this is a "blight clock": each released species accrues a species-specific enemy whose effectiveness grows with the species' cumulative abundance over time. Pressure in proportion to success reads as fair, a dominant species burns out faster, and the interface can say something concrete ("a parasite strain has specialised on your species; 34% infected"). The table ranks the candidate mechanisms as the researcher assessed them.

| Mechanism | Grounding | Recommended role |
|---|---|---|
| Specialist-enemy accumulation | Kill-the-winner, Janzen-Connell, Avida and Tierra parasites | Primary clock |
| Predator learning and prey switching | Frequency-dependent predation protects rare prey ([McCard et al.](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10530-021-02487-7)) | Cheap complement |
| Environmental drift and seasons | Fluctuation-dependent coexistence ([review](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.07926v1.pdf)) | Secondary; publish a forecast or it feels arbitrary |
| Resource overshoot | Paradox of enrichment ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradox_of_enrichment)) | Emergent punisher of over-efficient designs; hard to tune to a duration |
| Mutation load in small populations | Muller's ratchet and mutational meltdown ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muller%27s_ratchet)) | End-game finisher so a tiny population fades decisively |
| Hard season wipe | Path of Exile leagues of about 13 weeks ([PoE Wiki](https://www.poewiki.net/wiki/League)); Screeps seasons ([Screeps forum](https://screeps.com/forum/post/14704)) | Outer wrapper, not the per-species pressure |

Two cautions come from the same literature. Lively and colleagues warn that parasite-driven selection can maintain diversity while being nearly invisible in simple prevalence statistics, so the mechanism has to be surfaced in the interface or players will not perceive it ([Lively et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8692037)). And Xue and Goldenfeld showed that in a kill-the-winner model, demographic noise destroys coexistence through a cascade of extinctions unless enemies and prey coevolve ([Phys. Rev. Lett.](https://link.aps.org/accepted/10.1103/PhysRevLett.119.268101)); a world of 5 to 20 player species is exactly that small-number regime.

### The hazard must climb about 18-fold between day two and day six

A constant chance of extinction cannot produce a three-to-seven-day life. The researcher's own calculation shows why: a constant hazard with a five-day mean puts the 10th-to-90th percentile lifespan at about 0.5 to 11.5 days, too spread out to feel like a week and dominated by luck. To have 10% of species dead by day three and 90% by day seven, lifespans need roughly a Weibull distribution with shape near 3.6 and scale near 5.6 days, which means a hazard rising about as the 2.6th power of age, **around 18 times higher on day six than on day two**. Enemy adaptation that compounds with cumulative host-days produces that ramp naturally; a flat upkeep tax does not. This is arithmetic, not evidence that any particular parameter set yields the target, and no data was found on what values produce multi-day lifespans in any shipped artificial-life game.

Because populations are small and noisy, the researcher proposes tuning by feedback control: a global virulence coefficient adjusts automatically so the trailing median lifespan stays near target. Counter-play keeps the pressure soft. Following the Avida result that hosts escape parasites by acquiring new functions, a mid-life tweak partially resets enemy adaptation with diminishing returns, so skilled play extends life toward day seven and beyond without making immortality possible. For scoring, the researcher recommends ranking each release rather than each account, on a composite of lifespan and biomass-days (the area under the population curve), since peak biomass rewards boom-and-bust and pure lifespan rewards tiny hidden populations. No evidence was found on how players perceive ecology-driven decline compared with a hard cap; the fairness claim is inference.

### Designs should stay authored while the simulation changes only populations

The surveyed games sit at two poles. The Bibites and Species evolve everything and have no authorship; Thrive shields the player's species from automatic evolution, originally overriding simulation results for the player because the player "has no way to know what their editor changes would do" (per a low-authority community wiki draft), and later adding a prediction panel and raising the simulation's effect on the player's population from 20% to 50% ([Thrive Wiki talk page](https://thrive.fandom.com/wiki/Talk:Automatic_evolution); [patch 0.5.6.1](https://itch.io/devlog/323660/patch-0561.amp); [progress update](https://revolutionarygamesstudio.com/progress-update-01-25-2025/)). No retrieved game shows a successful middle where a designed species drifts freely yet stays the player's. Theory adds that free mutation degrades small asexual populations, and Tierra shows unconstrained evolution finds parasitism and cheating that undermine authored intent.

The two researchers who addressed this lean slightly differently. The prior-art researcher favours no in-simulation mutation plus a small owner-controlled patch budget; the systems researcher favours bounded micro-variation, where offspring jitter numeric parameters within a small envelope under the same budget, as the best fit for a Tamagotchi-like check-in. Both agree that structural changes must remain player-initiated. Spore and Thrive supply the template for owner edits: both restrict redesign to generation boundaries ([GameSpot](https://gamespot.com/articles/spore-exclusive-hands-on-from-cell-to-civilization/1100-6195809/)). Two further ideas are worth holding for later as unlocks rather than global rules: evolvability as a costed dial, after Polyworld's heritable mutation rate ([Yaeger](https://shinyverse.org/larryy/Yaeger.ALife3.pdf)), and sexual reproduction as a sidegrade that costs mate-finding but generates rare escape genotypes.

### One equal budget prices body and brain twice

Spore's two-limit pattern, a spend currency plus a separate complexity meter, is the model the researchers recommend: one cap prices power, the other protects legibility and server load ([Giant Bomb review](https://www.giantbomb.com/spore-creature-creator/3030-20746/user-reviews/2200-5254/)). Equal caps anchor fairness. Robocraft let players build freely under a CPU limit of 2,000 that did not rise with level ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robocraft)), and its later per-bay CPU progression from 750 to 2,000 shows how quickly a cap becomes a vertical progression lever once it is allowed to grow ([Robocraft devblog](https://robocraftgame.com/devblog/agility-and-cpu-progression-system)). Biology offers a rule for making body and brain commensurable: the expensive-tissue hypothesis holds that metabolically costly brain tissue was paid for by a smaller gut, though the hypothesis remains debated ([review](https://www.scielo.br/j/bjg/a/FxXZ7LPBDmZxjKKVbPym4Vb/)). The researchers' proposal is two-part pricing, a one-off build cost against the fixed cap plus an energy upkeep in the world, with brain functions cheap to buy and costly to run, so a large brain forces a better diet or a smaller gut.

For rebalancing, the researchers prefer ecological correction to price changes: extending the enemy system to parts, so a pathogen specialises on a common organ, taxes overused parts without touching the budget. If prices must move, Hearthstone Arena is the precedent for small data-driven adjustments from pick rate and win rate ([Blizzard](https://playhearthstone.com/en-us/blog/22788308)). With 5 to 20 players the live sample is tiny, so the realistic data source is automated batch simulation, in the manner of research systems that tune auto-battler cards by automated playtesting ([LUDUS review](https://liner.com/review/ludus-optimization-framework-to-balance-auto-battler-cards)). No live game was found that re-prices parts dynamically from play data.

### Unlocks should open niches, and the catalogue should belong to the group

The proven pattern for progression that does not make veterans stronger is variety rather than power. A fan analysis characterises Slay the Spire's meta-progression as unlocking more options, not more strength (developer intent unverified) ([game-mechanics wiki](https://saschb2b.github.io/game-mechanics/concepts/meta-as-variety-not-power)). Guild Wars 2 states it lacks a gear treadmill, and when ArenaNet added a higher gear tier many players treated it as a broken promise ([GW2 Wiki](https://wiki.guildwars2.com/wiki/Endgame); [PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/au/arenanet-guild-wars-2-gear-debate)). The documented risks of horizontal systems are different ones: players "quickly lose their goals" ([Mein-MMO](https://mein-mmo.de/en/3-things-that-all-other-mmorpgs-should-learn-from-guild-wars-2-and-3-mistakes-they-should-avoid,692897)), and Magic's head designer is reported to call complexity creep the biggest long-term danger to that game ([MTG Wiki](https://mtg.wiki/page/Complexity_creep), secondary source).

Discovery-triggered unlocks fit an ecosystem. Spore's creature stage unlocks parts by finding skeleton piles and by defeating or befriending other creatures ([Spore Wiki](https://spore.fandom.com/wiki/Creature_Stage)), and Cell Lab ties each new cell type to a named survival challenge ([namu.moe](https://namu.moe/w/%EC%85%80%EB%9E%A9)). The researchers propose unlock triggers drawn from what a species lived through ("survived a winter", "was parasitised for 48 hours", "went extinct to starvation"), so that **extinction pays out** and the designed-in death reads as progress. Each unlock should be priced at par in the shared budget and open a niche or a verb (a new resource, sense, signal or relationship) rather than raise a stat. Avida supports the niche framing: a diverse pool of limiting resources was sufficient on its own to produce and maintain distinct coexisting types ([Chow et al.](https://www.rifters.com/real/articles/Science_DigitalOrganisms.pdf)).

For a friend group, the researchers propose making discoveries group-wide after a short exclusivity window, so a late joiner inherits the whole catalogue; no retrieved game does exactly this. The researcher's own rough arithmetic puts six months of play at 30 to 75 atomic unlocks, with combinatorics doing most of the work (30 parts already give 435 pairs and 4,060 triples). Weekly world "mutators", such as a drought week or long nights, add variety with almost no authored content, following the Screeps practice of changing rules each season. No sourced figures exist on how much content sustains months of play.

### Relationships need identity signals, memory and something to trade

Rich relationships have emerged unprompted in digital evolution when organisms can interact through shared resources and each other's bodies, as in Tierra's sequence of parasites, hyper-parasites, social hosts and cheaters ([University of Delaware](https://www1.udel.edu/PR/Messenger/92/2/16.html)). Real ecology supplies mechanisms for relationships that develop over time. Cleaner-fish mutualism runs on reputation: client fish spent more time near cleaners they had seen cooperating, and cleaners fed more cooperatively when observed ([Bshary & Grutter](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1038/nature04755)). Mimicry is a signal economy in which a harmless mimic is protected only while rare relative to its model ([PMC6540699](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6540699/)).

The researchers' minimal mechanism set has three parts: a designer-chosen identity signal that other species' brains can sense, which enables search images, avoidance, warning coloration and mimicry; a small learned table per species of which signals have been good or bad for it; and at least two services one species can perform for another besides being eaten, with a temptation to cheat so that mutualisms can break. A relationship ledger then classifies each species pair by the sign of its effect on each side (predation, mutualism, commensalism, competition) and reports changes over time. The ecological classification is standard; using it as an interface ledger is the researchers' proposal, and no evidence shows players perceive emergent mutualisms without heavy interface support.

Legibility has shipped precedents. Shadow of Mordor's Nemesis system began as enemies simply remembering the player, was inspired by sports rivalries, and depends on hand-curating out dull outcomes ([MCV](https://mcvuk.com/development-news/monoliths-nemesis-system-inspired-by-real-world-sports/); [Game Informer](https://gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2017/09/22/the-unchained-chaos-of-middle-earth-shadow-of-war)). RimWorld's designer observes that players read more story into events than the mechanics contain ([Rock Paper Shotgun](https://www.rockpapershotgun.com/2016/08/12/how-rimworld-generates-great-stories)). Dwarf Fortress tracks only a small subset of its population as historical figures ([DF Wiki](https://dwarffortresswiki.org/index.php/Historical_figure)), and Species ships a clade diagram and a "web of life" view ([Steam](https://store.steampowered.com/app/774541?l=italian)). The recipe the researchers draw is a small cast of notable individuals, only consequential events, and a food-web graph whose edges change over time.

The world itself needs few ingredients. Avida found species richness peaked at intermediate productivity ([Chow et al.](https://www.rifters.com/real/articles/Science_DigitalOrganisms.pdf)), and enrichment is a documented route to boom-and-bust collapse, so the researchers recommend running the world as a chemostat with a fixed resource influx rather than large standing stocks: three or four separately renewing resources, two or three biomes with refuges, day/night and a season cycle on the order of a species' lifespan. Polyworld's "frenetic joggers", agents that simply ran straight ahead trying to eat and mate, show that degenerate strategies win in a featureless world ([Yaeger](https://shinyverse.org/larryy/Yaeger.ALife3.pdf)); patchy, seasonal and defended resources are what make brain budget pay. A background of non-player native species would fill the food web and absorb small-number noise. No source gives a validated minimal set, and none gives feasible world sizes or population counts for a hobby-scale server.

### A fifteen-minute visit should be recap, watch, one small change, notebook

Virtual-pet attachment comes from behaviour and responsibility, but the original Tamagotchi's rule that neglect means death caused enough distress that later models added a pause ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamagotchi_effect)). The better precedents make absence harmless or productive. Neko Atsume's developer aimed for a game enjoyable "without a significant investment of skill or time", and a critic describes its core as interesting things happening while the player is away ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neko_Atsume); [Ludus Novus](https://ludusnovus.net/2015/07/30/the-feline-grace-of-neko-atsume)). The original Digimon virtual pet made how the creature was treated select which adult form it became, with fallback forms when no requirement was met ([Wikimon](https://wikimon.net/Digital_Monster_Ver._1)). This game has a structural advantage here: species act autonomously, so a player who disappears for three days harms no one, unlike Neptune's Pride, where quitters are an acknowledged problem ([Iron Helmet forum](https://forum.ironhelmet.com/t/leavers-ruin-big-room-games-someone-is-going-to-get-a-massive-unfair-advantage-any-solutions-to-this/8301)).

The cadence precedents agree on restraint. Subterfuge's designers made "respecting players' time" a pillar and set out to minimise required daily check-ins ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/quality-of-life-in-subterfuge); [MCV](https://www.mcvuk.com/we-wanted-to-minimise-the-number-of-times-per-day-a-player-has-to-check-in/)), and even so one critic titled a piece "my favorite game of 2015, but I never want to play it again" ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/subterfuge-was-my-favorite-game-of-2015-but-i-never-want-to-play-it-again), title-level evidence). Wordle's one puzzle a day was designed to leave players wanting more, and its spoiler-free share grid, invented by a player, drove the social loop ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wordle)). Anything that makes more frequent checking advantageous will be felt as pressure in a friend group.

The researchers' proposed session is about three minutes of recap, four of live observation following one individual, three for one or two bounded actions, and five in a notebook or sandbox for the next design, with every step skippable and the recap alone a complete one-minute session. The bounded actions are one evolution point per day (a single parameter tweak or one part or card swap, applied to newborns), one nudge with a 24-hour cooldown that acts on the environment or signals and never puppeteers individuals, and naming a notable individual; unused allowances bank to two or three. The specific numbers are the researcher's own. The recap should carry three to five headlines ranked by consequence and tied to named individuals or species pairs, one annotated population sparkline, one relationship change, a forecast and exactly one decision prompt, plus a share card for the group chat. The weekly session should be player-pulled: an autopsy of the dead species, a fork of the old design or a friend's published one, edits in a draft, sandbox scenarios against a snapshot of today's ecosystem, a challenge puzzle for any newly unlocked block, then release, with no penalty for taking a day or two.

The researcher flags the central tension. If daily evolution points are strong, the best play becomes "launch anything, then patch daily", which devalues the weekly design; if they are weak, the daily session is hollow. A "plasticity reserve", budget left unspent at design time that funds later edits, lets each player choose a point on that spectrum. None of these edit policies has been evaluated empirically, and most care-loop precedents beyond those cited here could not be sourced.

## An all-TypeScript monolith on a small VPS covers every technical need

### A JavaScript renderer under DOM menus beats WebAssembly engines on phones

For a touch-heavy editor plus a zoomable 2D world, the researchers rank PixiJS v8 with a DOM interface first. PixiJS is actively maintained, with v8.23.0 released on 11 November 2026 and recent releases aimed at phones, including screen-sized pooled textures and per-backend code splitting ([PixiJS releases](https://github.com/pixijs/pixijs/releases)). Phaser 4, a full framework with a new WebGL renderer, shipped in April 2026 and stands at v4.2.1 ([Phaser releases](https://github.com/phaserjs/phaser/releases)); the researchers note a plausible risk, not evidenced, that coding agents lag on such a recent API. WebAssembly engines bring costs without benefit at this scale. Godot's own documentation says web exports "can run on mobile" but perform significantly worse than native, that C# projects cannot export to web, and that Safari has WebGL 2 issues other browsers lack ([Godot docs](https://github.com/godotengine/godot-docs/blob/master/tutorials/export/exporting_for_web.rst)); users report context loss and crashes on iOS across several versions ([Godot forum](https://forum.godotengine.org/t/webgl-context-loss-and-app-crash-in-godot-4-3-exported-web-on-ios-browsers/81024)). WebGPU now ships in Chrome for Android from version 121 and in Safari from version 26 ([MDN compat data](https://github.com/mdn/browser-compat-data/blob/main/api/GPU.json)), but coverage gaps remain and nothing in this design needs it, so WebGL2 stays the baseline. Sources conflict on whether PixiJS v8 selects WebGPU automatically; the renderer preference should be set explicitly.

The interface split is the researchers' inference: build the editor, recaps, lists and settings in the DOM, which gives text, scrolling and layout for free and is what coding agents handle best, and reserve the canvas for the world view and creature preview. No phone benchmark comparing PixiJS v8, Phaser 4 and plain Canvas2D was found; the only cross-engine benchmark located is a 2023 desktop test, and no authoritative bundle sizes were found either.

### A pure deterministic simulation package is the highest-leverage decision

TypeScript with struct-of-arrays typed-array storage is sufficient for the target scale and is the only option that trivially shares one simulation package between the browser sandbox and the server. In one benchmark, struct-of-arrays libraries such as bitECS iterate roughly three to ten times faster than object-based ones, while entity creation and destruction is their weak spot ([noctjs/ecs-benchmark](https://github.com/noctjs/ecs-benchmark), run on Node 17 with no hardware given). The researchers reason from this that at 10,000 agents and 10 ticks per second the budget is 10 microseconds per agent per tick, that raw iteration is negligible, and that the real costs are the behaviour interpreter and neighbour queries, which a uniform-grid spatial hash keeps cheap. The Bibites is the cautionary reference: per-object simulation in a general engine drops to single-digit frame rates as populations grow, and its developer plans a data-oriented rewrite ([Bibites 0.6.2 devlog](https://thebibites.itch.io/the-bibites/devlog/973592/the-bibites-062-steam-workshop-and-toward-the-future)). None of these throughput figures has been measured for this game.

Determinism is attainable with discipline. IEEE 754 guarantees identical results for basic arithmetic and square root but not for functions such as sine, and Rapier's documentation states that `Math.sin` and `Math.cos` are not cross-platform deterministic ([Rapier docs](https://rapier.rs/docs/user_guides/javascript/determinism)); a lockstep study achieved consistent runs across browsers and CPU architectures after replacing sine with a custom approximation ([arXiv 1705.09704](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1705.09704)). The recommended shape is a pure package with a step function, no I/O, no wall-clock time, a seeded random generator stored in world state, a fixed timestep, and lookup tables for trigonometry. That one package then runs in a Web Worker for the design sandbox, on the server for the real world, and headless for tests, fast-forward balance runs and replays.

Behaviour programs should run in a tiny deterministic interpreter with an instruction budget, never as raw JavaScript. Screeps runs arbitrary player code and therefore needed a separate V8 isolate per player ([Screeps changelog](https://blog.screeps.com/2018/03/changelog-2018-03-05/)); a rule-card language interpreted as data avoids that whole class of sandboxing problem. Screeps also supplies the tick structure worth copying, in which all agents read the same state and emit intents that a second stage resolves ([Screeps docs](https://docs.screeps.com/architecture.html)). Two further lessons come from open-source references: ALIEN conserves energy across everything in its world, which the researchers recommend copying so populations self-limit, and ships a headless mode ([chrxh/alien](https://github.com/chrxh/alien)); Thrive keeps tuning in JSON data files separate from code ([Thrive](https://github.com/Revolutionary-Games/Thrive)).

### One always-on process is cheaper and safer than serverless actors

A single Node or Bun process with WebSockets and SQLite satisfies "a world that ticks around the clock for 5 to 20 players". The table gives the researchers' three candidate stacks; costs are reasoned from search-summary prices and none of the stacks has been validated by measurement.

| Stack | Shape | Monthly cost (reasoned) | Main weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. TypeScript monolith (recommended) | One process: tick loop, WebSocket server, SQLite; static PixiJS and DOM client; PWA | About €6 to €10 | Developer owns deploys, backups, TLS and reconnection code |
| B. Stack A plus Colyseus 0.18 | Colyseus for rooms, reconnection, auth hooks; custom binary snapshots | €6 to €10 self-hosted; from $15 on Colyseus Cloud | Pre-1.0 API churn; abstractions aimed at short-lived match rooms |
| C1. SpacetimeDB 2.x | TypeScript module with a scheduled tick; clients subscribe to queries | $0 to $25, with free-tier figures that conflict between two vendor pages | Every world mutation is a transactional row write; BSL licence; new TypeScript runtime |
| C2. Cloudflare Durable Object | One object as the world, alarms as heartbeat | A base fee plus a few dollars | 128 MB single-threaded isolate; eviction; usage billing that punishes bugs |

Hetzner's small cloud servers cost €5.49 to €5.99 a month after two price rises in 2026 ([Hetzner](https://docs.hetzner.com/general/infrastructure-and-availability/price-adjustment/)). Colyseus 0.18 is well maintained, with core 0.18.28 released on 10 November 2026, and self-hosting is free ([Colyseus releases](https://github.com/colyseus/colyseus/releases); [pricing](https://colyseus.io/pricing)). Platform risk is real: Hathora's game hosting shut down in May 2026 ([Edgegap](https://edgegap.com/comparison/edgegap-vs-hathora)), and posts report five-figure Durable Objects bills from runaway alarm loops, one attributed to AI-written code, though only the titles were seen ([aiidelist](https://aiidelist.com/blog/cloudflare-10811-bill-codex-durable-objects-alarm-loop)). Fixed-price hosting means an agent-written bug costs CPU rather than money.

For spectating, clients never simulate the shared world; they interpolate server snapshots, a pattern with an open library that buffers about three server frames ([geckosio/snapshot-interpolation](https://github.com/geckosio/snapshot-interpolation)). The researchers estimate that 200 visible creatures at 8 bytes each and 4 snapshots a second is about 6.4 kB per second before delta encoding, and that WebSockets suffice because spectating at low tick rates has no need for unreliable transport. For 5 to 20 friends, an invite code plus a passkey or emailed magic link is enough authentication.

### A PWA is viable for a friend group, with known iOS gaps

iOS supports Web Push for web apps added to the Home Screen since 16.4, and only there ([WebKit](https://webkit.org/?p=13878)); Declarative Web Push arrived in Safari 18.4 ([WebKit](https://webkit.org/?p=16535)); and from iOS 26 every site added to the Home Screen opens as a web app by default ([MacRumors](https://www.macrumors.com/2025/06/18/safari-changes-on-ios-26/)). The remaining gaps are that each friend must add the app to the Home Screen manually before push can be requested, that developers report push subscriptions silently disappearing ([webscraft](https://webscraft.org/blog/pwa-pushspovischennya-na-ios-u-2026-scho-realno-pratsyuye?lang=en)), and that iOS has no Vibration API ([bugnet](https://bugnet.io/blog/fix-web-game-vibrate-api-not-available-ios-safari)). None of these blocks a check-in-and-spectate game, but push needs pairing with an in-app recap. No quantitative data on iOS push reliability was found. One source claims Apple removed standalone web apps in the EU with iOS 17.4; one researcher recalls, without verification, that Apple reversed this before release, so it needs checking if any player is in the EU. If the gaps hurt, Capacitor 8 wraps the same build ([Ionic](https://ionic.io/blog/announcing-capacitor-8)), with TestFlight builds expiring every 90 days ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/help/app-store-connect/test-a-beta-version/overview-of-testflight/)).

## Claude Code builds web games well when it can check its own work

### Polished agent-built games took weeks of human-directed iteration

By late 2026 there are many publicly playable browser games built almost entirely by Claude Code. Simon Willison wrote in October that four projects "have convinced me that the models are now capable of producing fun games with very little guidance" ([Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Oct/28/vibe-game-development/)). His Battle Drones took 12 major iterations and 321 agent commits; the first version was workable but unenjoyable, and the game improved after the blunt prompt "Make it a LOT more fun" and sustained play-driven iteration ([Battle Drones](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Oct/20/battledrones/)). A Mario Galaxy-style game posted to Hacker News took **731 commits over 53 days** with about 95% of the code written by Claude Code, and its author accumulated custom planning and review skills and a 164-line CLAUDE.md of hard constraints learned from debugging ([Show HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47600002)). The winner of a 2026 jam requiring 90% AI-written code was reportedly built with Claude Code in about two weeks by an experienced iOS developer running two or three sessions in parallel ([Wccftech](https://wccftech.com/ios-developer-made-capybara-game-using-vibe-coding-and-won-25000/)).

Anthropic's own experiment sets the floor. A single-agent run on a game-maker prompt (20 minutes, $9) produced a broken game in which nothing responded to input; a planner, generator and evaluator harness (6 hours, $200) produced a playable one that still had physics issues the evaluator missed ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/harness-design-long-running-apps)). In every account the human acted as director and playtester. The agent struggled most with game feel, camera work and judging its own output; Battle Drones' replays desynced because Chrome and Safari compute trigonometric functions slightly differently, according to a secondary write-up ([explainx.ai](https://explainx.ai/blog/battle-drones-simon-willison-vibe-coded-rts-audio-multiplayer-claude-code-321-commits-2026)). Every polished example found uses the web stack. Two gaps matter for this project: no source describes an agent-built persistent, server-authoritative ecosystem simulation, and no first-hand account of a solo Claude Code game project lasting six months or more was found. The case-study details come from snippets, since the primary posts were not read in full.

### Give the agent a check it can run, and put absolute rules in hooks

Anthropic's current guidance was read in full, and it reduces to three moves. First, give Claude a pass/fail check: "Claude stops when the work looks done. If it has nothing to run that tells it whether the work actually is done, 'looks done' is all it has to go on" ([Best practices](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)). Second, keep CLAUDE.md to what Claude cannot learn from the code: exact test, typecheck and lint commands, places where the code is misleading, decisions not in the repository, and anything corrected twice already, in under 200 lines. Third, enforce the few absolute rules deterministically: "If you keep adding emphasis to a CLAUDE.md line and Claude still misses it, that's a sign the line belongs in a hook." A Stop hook that exits with code 2 sends Claude back to work until a check passes. The same page marks much older advice as obsolete, including treating the context window as the main constraint, capital-letter emphasis, "think hard" phrases and describing architecture in CLAUDE.md, and notes that subagents give parallelism or a fresh reviewer "but not a smarter answer".

A phone-driven workflow is practical within specific limits. Cloud sessions run on Anthropic-managed Linux virtual machines with about 4 vCPUs and 16 GB of memory, with Node, pnpm and Chrome for Testing preinstalled, and they continue after the device is closed; the mobile app is a client for such sessions rather than a place where code runs ([Cloud environments](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/cloud-environments); [Mobile](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/mobile)). A foreground command waits two minutes by default and up to ten, then may run in the background for up to thirty more, which bounds how large a balance sweep fits in one command. A single-repository cloud session loads the repository's CLAUDE.md, hooks, MCP servers and skills. Projects, the public-beta feature that runs parallel threads per task, load CLAUDE.md "but not its skills, .mcp.json servers, or .claude/settings.json", so repository hooks do not run there, and they consume plan limits faster ([Projects](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/claude-projects)). The researchers infer that any check a Stop hook performs should be duplicated in continuous integration, which Project threads do respond to through auto-fix on pull requests.

### Verification needs a browser, a sceptical reviewer and a headless simulation

Without browser testing, Claude marked features complete that did not work end to end, and giving it browser tools "dramatically improved performance" ([Anthropic, 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-harnesses-for-long-running-agents)). Self-evaluation is unreliable: agents tend toward "confidently praising the work—even when, to a human observer, the quality is obviously mediocre", and tuning a separate evaluator to be sceptical proved "far more tractable" than making a generator self-critical ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/harness-design-long-running-apps)). Canvas games add a wrinkle, since a canvas has no DOM to query; third-party skills recommend screenshots plus seeded randomness, frozen time and a fixed viewport, and warn that headless WebGL canvases can screenshot blank (snippet-level sources). For balance, a Hacker News discussion favoured cheap headless Monte Carlo simulation over LLM "players" for deterministic games ([HN summary](https://ai-paper-delta.vercel.app/en/papers/hn_47947525)).

The researchers' inference is that an ecosystem simulation is unusually well suited to agent verification, because its core can be a pure function of state, seed and tick. That supports three layers of checking: deterministic simulation tests and golden replays; a debug hook that returns game state as JSON so the agent can assert on it after driving the interface; and phone-viewport screenshots reviewed by a fresh subagent against written criteria. Playwright's device presets emulate phone viewports and touch but are not a real device ([Playwright docs](https://github.com/microsoft/playwright/blob/main/docs/src/emulation.md)). Screenshots cannot judge feel, so animation timing, touch ergonomics and readability on a real phone remain the developer's job.

### Art as code is consistent; Claude's design judgement needs outside checks

Evidence on art pipelines is the weakest in the research. What is documented is that agent-built games commonly use code-generated visuals and audio, and that tiny parameter-array sound generators such as ZzFX keep audio in the repository as text ([zzfx](https://npmjs.com/package/zzfx)). Anthropic improved Claude's visual design output by grading it against explicit criteria, weighting design quality and originality above craft and functionality ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/harness-design-long-running-apps)). The researchers infer that a parts-based creature designer favours procedural art, where a shared palette, stroke width and eye style guarantee coherence, and that a style-sheet page rendering every part side by side gives a reviewer something to grade. **No source describes Claude-generated procedural creature art in a shipped game, and no evidence exists on how well current models judge "cute".** The agent cannot hear, so the developer must audition sound.

On game design, two weaknesses are well evidenced. A study summarised by Wharton found that a chatbot improves individual ideas but "significantly reduces the diversity of ideas within a group of ideas" ([Wharton](https://mackinstitute.wharton.upenn.edu/?p=33528)), and Anthropic documents lenient self-assessment. Direct evidence on language models as systems or balance designers is thin, so the techniques are inference: supply the distinctive constraints yourself and ask for many varied options; never ask the designing session whether its design is good; use a fresh session with a hostile brief such as "find the dominant creature build and prove it with simulation output"; and let the agent write search code over creature designs against the headless simulator, which yields evidence rather than opinion.

### The web is the only target an agent can build and ship from a phone

Agent tooling for native mobile is mature on a developer's own Mac, through Expo's official MCP server and Xcode build servers ([Expo](https://docs.expo.dev/eas/ai/mcp)), but none of it transfers to Anthropic's cloud sandbox, which is x86 Linux with a base image that cannot be replaced. People do ship Claude-built iOS apps, with App Store review remaining a manual pain point; one developer who wrote no code reported three rejections before going live ([dev.to](https://dev.to/asvid/look-at-me-im-the-ios-developer-now-icj)). The inference is direct: a web app, optionally installable as a PWA, is the only target the agent can build, run, screenshot and deploy entirely from cloud sessions started on a phone. If the game is ever wrapped natively, Apple's rule against apps executing downloaded code that changes functionality is worth checking against player-programmed behaviour ([TechRepublic](https://www.techrepublic.com/article/news-apple-vibe-coding-app-store-rules/)); rule cards interpreted as data by the app's own engine are a different thing from downloaded code, but that reading was not confirmed against Apple's text.

### At runtime, Claude should narrate computed facts and stay out of the simulation

Cost is not the obstacle. From Anthropic's price list, Claude Haiku 5.5 costs $0.10 per million input tokens and $0.50 per million output tokens, and the Batch API halves both ([Pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing)). The researcher's arithmetic for 20 players receiving three summaries a day (1,800 calls a month at 3,000 tokens in and 400 out) comes to **about $0.90 a month on Haiku 5.5**, $18 on Sonnet 5.5 and $36 on Opus 5.5 before the batch discount. Structured outputs constrain responses to a schema, with stated limits such as no recursive schemas ([Structured outputs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/structured-outputs)).

The risks are reception and trust. An analysis of 132 Steam reviews of Vaudeville, a game with LLM-generated dialogue, found hallucinations and weak character memory as recurring complaints ([CONVERSATIONS'23 paper](https://samucox.github.io/files/CONVERSATIONS'23%20Paper.pdf)), and Vapor World launched to mostly negative reviews after AI-generated cutscenes replaced in-engine ones ([Everything Edinburgh](https://everythingedinburgh.com/games/news/vapor-world-steam-ai-cutscene-backlash/)). Those failures come from letting the model invent facts. The researchers' ordering of uses by fit starts with a nightly "nature documentary" recap written from a structured event log, then names and flavour text generated once and stored, then a "why did my species die" coach fed the simulator's own cause-of-death data, then natural language to rule cards through a strict schema validated by the game, then pre-reviewed weekly events. Player-chosen creature names flow into prompts and must be treated as untrusted text. The API key belongs on the server only, with a monthly spend cap. No evidence was found on how players receive low-stakes generated recaps, and whether structured outputs work inside the Batch API was not confirmed.

### The documented risks are open databases, drift and false "done"

The best-evidenced danger in agent-built apps is security. A scan of 5,600 publicly deployed AI-built apps reportedly found more than 2,000 high-impact vulnerabilities and 400 exposed secrets, and a scan of 1,645 apps on one platform found about 10% with fully exposed databases; the search summaries did not tie each figure to a single page ([Cloud Security Alliance](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-ai-generated-code-security-vibe-coding-202); [Noqta](https://noqta.tn/en/blog/vibe-coding-security-gaps-2026)). For this project the inference is that a small server that owns all writes, with no database keys in client code, removes that class of failure. Architecture quality is the slower risk: a 2026 preprint finds agents leave a distinct pattern of technical debt that functional correctness hides ([arXiv 2605.02741](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.02741v1)), and practitioners recommend constraints enforced in continuous integration over prose. Anthropic's own documents add operational cautions: auto mode "is a classifier model judging each action rather than a hard rule... it can be wrong", and a stated boundary "can get lost when the conversation compacts" ([Best practices](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)); its long-running harness pattern includes the rule that "it is unacceptable to remove or edit tests" ([Anthropic, 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-harnesses-for-long-running-agents)).

## Recommended directions, open questions and first spikes

### Behaviour editor: mode pages of rule cards, tapped into place

Build the editor as two to six named modes, each holding at most six to eight priority-ordered WHEN→DO cards, with "switch to mode" as an ordinary action. Use one semantics throughout: conditions are states re-evaluated each think tick, actions are sustained while their card wins, and the highest true card controls the body. Author by tapping slots and choosing from a bottom sheet filtered to legal, unlocked blocks; reserve dragging for reordering cards. Ship drives as built-in sensors, two or three signal colours, target filters ("nearest female kin", "weakest prey", "my offspring"), a flag block and a timer block, and hide steering weights inside parameterised actions such as "stay with group". Make the explanation views part of the first version, not polish: the thought bubble, the "why not" readout, firing counts with shadowed-card warnings, and the post-mortem autopsy. Start players from presets they tweak, then let them reorder, add cards, add modes, and finally use signals, flags and timers, each step a superset of the last. The "neural" identity can live in the fiction and presentation (drives, a brain that visibly lights up the winning card) rather than in weighted synapses; that reframing is this report's suggestion, and the evidence that weighted graphs are hard to hand-author and hard to read is what motivates it.

### Creature style: rung-two soft critters, vector parts, a cheated top-down camera

Move away from the microbe look to soft round critters with faces, built from layered vector parts drawn by code, with verlet-chain ears, tails and antennae, squash-and-stretch bodies and stub legs that bob. Draw faces toward the viewer on top-down bodies, set the world in a tide pool, pond or terrarium, and add a front-facing close-up screen for the Tamagotchi moment. Bake one texture per species for mid zoom and fall back to dots when zoomed out. Let individual parts opt into rung-three behaviour later (a real chain tail, two to six stepping legs), and treat side-view articulated animals as out of scope. Body parts must alter simulation outcomes continuously (speed, energy cost, reach, sensing range), and organs competing for body volume is the mechanism to test for forcing trade-offs by geometry.

### Lifespan pressure and progression: a blight clock under a fixed cap

Make specialist-enemy accumulation the primary lifespan pressure, visible in the interface from the first day of a species' life, with a hazard that ramps steeply with cumulative success and a virulence coefficient auto-tuned toward a five-day median. Add a published seasonal forecast as a secondary pressure and a small-population finisher so declines end decisively. Wrap the world in seasons of four to eight weeks that reset the map and vary the rules. Score each release on lifespan plus biomass-days. Keep designs authored: begin with clonal species whose only changes are the owner's rate-limited edits applied to newborns, and treat bounded numeric jitter as an experiment once the clock is tuned, since the researchers split on this point. Give every player the same combined body-and-brain budget, charge build cost against the cap and upkeep in the world, price sensors rather than rules, and keep a separate complexity cap. Make every unlock a niche or a verb priced at par, trigger unlocks from what species live through including their extinction, share the catalogue across the group after a short exclusivity window, and teach each new block through a Cell Lab-style challenge puzzle.

### Daily and weekly loop: absence is free, and death starts the fun part

Design for one satisfying daily visit: a curated recap that is complete in a minute, live watching with the thought bubble, at most one evolution point and one environmental nudge, and a notebook with a successor design in incubation. Never punish absence, send one daily digest by default, and keep event notifications opt-in and rare. Make the weekly session player-pulled and structured as autopsy, fork, draft, sandbox, challenge, release. Hide the brains of living species from rivals and reveal them at extinction, which preserves design secrecy and gives each death a social payoff.

### Technology stack: Stack A, with every escape hatch deferred

Use Vite and TypeScript, PixiJS v8 on WebGL2 for the world and creature preview, a DOM framework of the developer's choice for the editor and recaps, and a PWA with Web Push. Put the simulation in a pure deterministic package with typed-array storage, a seeded generator, a fixed timestep of 5 to 10 ticks a second and a bytecode interpreter for rule cards with an instruction budget. Run one Node or Bun process on a fixed-price VPS with a tick loop, WebSockets, binary area-of-interest snapshots at 2 to 5 a second, and SQLite for accounts, designs, event log and world snapshots. Defer Colyseus, Rust and WebAssembly, WebGPU, and a Capacitor wrapper until a measured problem calls for one.

### Development workflow: tests the agent cannot weaken, playtests only the developer can do

Structure the repository as a small workspace of simulation, protocol, server and client packages. Name two commands in a CLAUDE.md kept under 200 lines: one that runs the headless simulation tests and one that produces phone-viewport screenshots. Add a Stop hook for typecheck and tests, mirror those checks in continuous integration with branch protection on the main branch, and deploy from the pipeline so credentials never enter an agent session. Commit a scope document that lists what is out, a feature list with pass flags and a decisions log, and have Claude interview the developer into a written spec before large features. Record procedures such as launching the app or running a balance sweep as skills. Use fresh-context review for the simulation core, the auth and data layer, and every schema migration, and use hostile fresh sessions plus search over designs to hunt dominant strategies. Enforce one or two machine-checked boundaries, starting with a rule that the simulation package imports nothing from interface or network code. Use plain cloud sessions from the phone for single tasks, and Projects for parallel work only where continuous integration covers what hooks would have enforced. Reserve the developer's own time for what no agent can judge: playing the build on a real phone and deciding whether it is fun and cute.

### The main open questions

The largest unknown is human: whether adult non-programmer friends can author mode pages of rule cards on a phone, since no study covers that population and screen size. Close behind is expressiveness, because the constructions for packs, mating rituals and nesting grounds are untested and nobody knows how well players grasp scent-mark signalling. The lifespan clock is unproven: no parameter data exists for any comparable game, small populations are noisy, and no evidence shows whether players perceive ecology-driven decline as fair or even notice it. The strength of daily edits against the weekly redesign is a balance question with no precedent data. Relationships may stay invisible without heavy interface support. Performance is unmeasured at every layer, from milliseconds per tick at 10,000 agents to frames per second with hundreds of critters on a mid-range phone, and no source gives feasible world sizes for a hobby server. iOS push reliability is unquantified. Whether Claude-authored procedural parts reach "cute" is unknown, as is whether an agent-built codebase stays maintainable past six months. Content volume and complexity creep have no sourced guidance. And the claim that no similar game exists remains provisional.

### The first spikes, in order of the risk they retire

The first spike is a headless simulation with a toy rule-card interpreter and grid spatial hash at 10,000 agents, measured in milliseconds per tick on the intended class of server. It also establishes the agent workflow, since its tests become the checks in CLAUDE.md, the Stop hook and the pipeline. The second is a phone prototype of the card editor with tap-to-place slots and a test tank, used to attempt the six target behaviours and then put in front of two or three friends; this tests the heart of the game before anything is built around it. The third uses the first spike's fast-forward mode to tune the blight clock, running many seeds to see whether any parameter set yields the three-to-seven-day distribution without cascading collapse. The fourth is the creature look: one rung-two critter with a face, chain attachments and squash-and-stretch in PixiJS, then 300 on screen at three levels of detail on a mid-range Android phone and an iPhone in Safari. The fifth is the spectate pipeline and PWA together: binary snapshots at 4 a second with interpolation over mobile data, installation on iOS 26 and Android, and a week of push delivery observed. The sixth is a recap generated in a nightly batch from a structured event log, shown to friends to learn whether they open it and trust it.

## Conclusion

One artefact does five jobs in this design. The pure, deterministic simulation with its small rule interpreter is the heart of the game, the agent's verification harness, the balance laboratory, the tuning rig for the lifespan clock and the engine of the player's sandbox. That is why the first spike is worth more than its size suggests, and why the stack decision that matters most is not the renderer or the host but keeping that package free of everything else. It also explains why this game is an unusually good fit for agent-driven development: most of what makes it work can be checked without anyone looking at a screen.

What cannot be checked that way is where the project will succeed or fail. The open questions that matter are whether friends can write rule cards on a phone, whether a species' decline feels earned, whether the creatures are cute, and whether the morning recap gets opened. The research shows that agents grade their own work leniently, that screenshots cannot convey feel, and that every polished agent-built game had a human playing it throughout. The scarce input is therefore the developer's own play and the friends' reactions, and the plan should spend that input early, on the editor prototype, before investing in the world around it. The shift in understanding is that legibility, not neural realism, is what makes behaviour programming the heart of a game: the player has to see which rule fired, why the species died, and what the death unlocked.
